# Far ideas, lens: geometry, continuous spaces and visualisation (2026-11-13)

Draft, not independently checked. Grades on each statement: measured (a script in `far-ideas-geometry/` produced it, toys only), derived, cited, guessed. Every board in the probes is a generated toy (painter of gap 6a and 6b, rim colours `cf`, inner colours `ci`, one corner pinned); nothing here is Eternity II progress.

The bottom line first. I examined eighteen geometric framings and tested two tonight. A literal projection of the solution space and of the search tree (probe A) shows nothing: solution prefixes sit scattered inside clouds whose shape is the tree's first branchings, and geometric scores give AUCs of 0.46 to 0.53 on average over five toy boards. A flow inside the Birkhoff polytope (probe B) never solves, in 30 runs on six boards. Its soft point concentrates on "boards whose edges match", which search already uses. Against near-solutions far from every solution it shows no edge over boards found by annealing and about 2 sd over boards sampled by DFS, on 5x5 only, with a confound not removed: at best a D4-sized tilt, about 1 bit. Geometry built from local matching inherits O3. Only ideas that put the each-tile-once rule into the geometry itself (a lifted semidefinite point, the topology of near-solution level sets) are left with some uncertainty. My odds are under 1 percent for 10 bits on each of them.

## 1. Eighteen ideas, three lines each

Format: the idea / why it might see what search does not / the first reason it probably fails. Where a ROUTES row exists I name it.

1. **Birkhoff polytope plus a quadratic flow (graduated assignment, soft-assign).** Placements are relaxed to a doubly stochastic point (each cell and each tile sums to 1), the expected number of matched edges is raised as a quadratic form, and a temperature pushes the point toward a vertex. / It is global from the first step: every tile's mass feels every cell at once, and the each-tile-once rule is in the constraint, not checked afterwards. / Measured tonight (probe B): 0 of 30 runs solved on six boards from 5x5 to 7x7. The vertex reached matches 82 to 92 percent of the edges and is as far from the nearest solution as from a random board. The soft point favours solutions over random boards (3 to 5 sd). On 5x5 it does not favour them over annealed near-solutions, and favours them over DFS-sampled near-solutions by about 2 sd. Closest rows: LP relaxation, difference map. The difference here is an interior flow, not an LP vertex or a projection pair; the outcome is the same.

2. **Lasserre / sum-of-squares lift: the solution as a rank-one point of a moment matrix.** Index a PSD matrix by placements (cell, tile, rotation), impose the assignment and matching constraints on its entries, and round from the leading eigenvector. / PSD couples all pairs of placements globally, and for quadratic assignment the SDP bound (Zhao, Karisch, Rendl, Wolkowicz 1998, cited, not opened) is much tighter than LP or Gilmore-Lawler bounds. It is the only relaxation in this list where each-tile-once enters as a second-moment condition. / Degree-2 linearisation was blind on 4x4 and 5x5 toys (gap 6a). On the 16x16 there are about 2^17.3 rim-consistent placements, so the matrix has about 2^17.3 rows (derived), and quadratic assignment SDPs stop near n = 30 to 40 in the literature. Developed below as idea B.

3. **Embedding partial boards, with the solution at the centre, at an extreme or in a dense region.** Each live node at depth d becomes a point; PCA, centroid overlap and one-cell-neighbour density are computed; then we ask where the prefixes of solutions sit. / A cheap statistic of the level cloud (sampled by probes) that ranks true prefixes high would be a tilt in D4's sense, and geometry is a different family from D4's census statistics. / Measured tonight (probe A): on four toy boards the AUC of solution prefixes against dead prefixes sits between 0.37 and 0.66, with no consistent sign. The PCA picture shows only the tree's first branchings. Developed below as idea A.

4. **The shape of the solution set (replica overlap, backbone, clustering).** If solutions formed one cluster with a backbone, aiming at the cluster's centre (marginals) would locate them. / Belief-propagation decimation works exactly when that holds (random k-SAT below the condensation point). / Measured: on a 5x5 board with 940 solutions the mean distance between two solutions is 20.1 cells of 25 and the backbone is the pinned cell only. On a 6x6 board with 14 solutions they come in small families (pairs at 4 to 12 cells) placed at random distances (24 to 34 of 36). With five clues the real puzzle has about one solution in expectation, so its "centre" is the solution itself and there is nothing to average.

5. **Topology of near-solution level sets (connectivity, persistent homology over the mismatch count).** Build the graph of complete boards with at most k mismatches, joined by a swap or rotation. Then ask in which component the solutions sit as k grows, and when they join the giant component. / This states exactly what local search needs, a path that never exceeds k, and a topological obstruction would be a theorem rather than a failed run. / Path 4's empty band (no board with 10 or fewer mismatches at 65 to 160 changed cells, argued) already predicts the answer: the solution is an isolated point up to large k. A positive here is a road for local search, which is priced out. Developed below as idea C, mainly as a way to get a negative of proof grade.

6. **Discrete Morse theory on the mismatch landscape.** Count critical cells (local minima, saddles) of the mismatch function on the swap graph; Morse inequalities bound the Betti numbers of the level sets. / It gives counts of basins without visiting them. / Counting critical cells is as hard as enumeration, and the useful output, the basin of the solution, is idea 5's question with worse constants.

7. **Optimal transport from the tile multiset to the cell demands (Wasserstein balance per region).** Each region's boundary demands a colour histogram; a transport plan from unused tiles to regions must exist; its cost is a bound. / Transport is global and cheap (network flow), and it sees counts across the whole board at once. / These are the integer balance laws of gap 5 in another notation (prefix conservation LP: 0 bits before depth 192, independently re-checked), and the colour-ledger chi-square row (path 4, at most about 1.7 orders).

8. **Height functions and tiling groups (Thurston, Conway and Lagarias).** Give each colour a generator of a group. A region is tileable only if its boundary word is a product of tile words. For dominoes and lozenges this gives height functions and exact tileability. / For simply connected regions it turns a global tileability question into a word problem on the boundary, which is the kind of non-local invariant O3 asks for. / With free rotation and 22 colours on four sides, each tile is a closed loop of four colour letters. The tile group is generated by relations that make it essentially abelian on the colour counts (derived sketch: the four rotations of a tile give its letters cyclically, and a tile's word is trivial on matched edges because each letter meets its inverse). What remains is the colour balance of gap 5. Each-tile-once has no expression as a group relation. Guessed odds of a non-abelian residue: under 1 in 1,000.

9. **Spectral coordinates of the tile compatibility graph (eigenvectors as positions, seriation, as in jigsaw and genome assembly).** Tiles are vertices, weighted by how many sides could match; the Laplacian's low eigenvectors are tried as (x, y) coordinates. / In jigsaw solvers and seriation the true geometry is the dominant low-frequency structure of the affinity graph. / Row A1 (spectral pair statistics, killed: below colour rarity, z -7.94). Here the affinity depends on colours only. Two tiles share a colour with probability set by the counts, independent of their distance in the solution, so the graph is a random graph with a planted grid of weight about 1/degree. Its spectrum is the random-graph bulk (derived).

10. **Tropical geometry of the score.** Tropicalise the polynomial whose monomials are boards weighted by matched edges. Its Newton polytope's vertices are the score maxima; the tropical variety is where two boards tie. / Tropical methods find the maximiser by polyhedral geometry, without enumeration. / The tropical maximiser of a sum over permutations of products of pair weights is the quadratic assignment maximum. The tropicalisation adds nothing over the LP or the Birkhoff face (idea 1), and computing the Newton polytope is the enumeration.

11. **Toric ideals and Gröbner bases: the solution as a point of a toric variety.** Encode placements as monomials and matching as binomial relations, then read solutions off a Gröbner basis. / Algebraic geometry has solved combinatorial existence questions without search. / Gap 6a: linearisation needs degree 3 to 4 even on 4x4 toys and is memory-bound on the 16x16 (independently re-checked). This is that row.

12. **The puzzle as a planar map: Whitney embedding uniqueness.** A 3-connected planar graph has a unique embedding, so if the adjacency graph of the solution were known, positions would follow. Choose, among all colour-compatible tile pairs, the subgraph that is a 16x16 grid. / Planarity and 3-connectivity are global, linear-time-checkable properties, a family of tests unlike edge matching. / Selecting the grid inside the candidate graph is subgraph isomorphism, the original problem. Planarity tests apply only once a subgraph is fixed, which means after search.

13. **Rigidity, tensegrity, spring embedders.** Tiles become rigid squares and colour matches become springs, with hard-core repulsion for each-tile-once. Relax, and read the solution as the zero-energy configuration. / Force-directed layouts recover hidden geometry in sparse graphs, and rigidity theory states when a frame has a unique realisation. / This is idea 1 with a different energy. A continuous relaxation of an assignment problem has exponentially many local minima, and the zero-energy state is the solution itself, which the springs see no better than local search does.

14. **Fourier analysis on the symmetric group (low-order representations).** Represent a distribution over permutations by its low-order Fourier coefficients (Kondor, Huang and Guestrin's inference over permutations, cited, not opened). Pairwise matching is a second-order function, so it lives in a few low irreducibles. / It handles each-tile-once exactly, at a resolution chosen by the order. / Order-k Fourier coefficients are the k-wise marginals: the Sherali-Adams level-k LP in another basis. LP is blind until depth 192, and order k costs n^(2k).

15. **Analog dynamics: oscillator or coherent Ising machines, continuous-time solvers (Ercsey-Ravasz and Toroczkai).** The constraint system is mapped onto ODEs whose attractors are solutions; auxiliary "memory" variables grow on unsatisfied constraints to escape traps. / These solvers report polynomial analog time on some hard SAT families. The memory term is not a temperature, so the dynamics differs from annealing. / On planted, critically constrained instances the analog time to solve grows exponentially (their own papers, cited from memory, unverified). On a digital simulator this is local search with weights, the priced-out family.

16. **Frontier trajectories in colour-count space.** As row scan proceeds, the open frontier's colour histogram traces a path in Z^22. The solution's path should stay near the line set by the remaining tiles' supply. / It is a one-dimensional geometric object per node, and it is cheap. / This is the colour-ledger prune (path 4 1c, priced at most about 1.7 orders) and gap 5's balance laws.

17. **Renormalisation: coarse-grain 2x2 blocks into macro-tiles and look for a fixed point of the block map.** Each scale has its own colour count, and at a fixed point the board solves itself recursively. / Hierarchical structure is how aperiodic tilings are proved and built. / Macro-tiles are a priced row (pattern databases, macro-tiles). The block set explodes: each tile is used once across all blocks, and macro edges carry pairs of colours, roughly squaring the colour count. The coarse instance is no easier per bit (guessed).

18. **Visualise the whole tree as a branching surface (hyperbolic layout) and look for the solution's path.** Draw the row-scan tree in the Poincaré disc, colour each node by its subtree size, and see whether the solution's branch has a visible shape (straighter, wider, earlier). / A picture can reveal a regularity no preregistered statistic was written for. / The shape the picture would show is the level widths. Those match the first-moment model to 0.03 digits (measured, Track 10), and the solution's branch is size-biased by a factor the model predicts (D4: about 1 bit). Probe A is the low-dimensional version, and it shows nothing.

## 2. The three I would bet on, one page each

"Bet on" means where some uncertainty is left, not that I expect a gain. Each needs 10 or more bits off 2^134.4; the program's prior is under 1 percent per idea, and mine below agrees.

### Idea A. A geometric tilt on the live level (centroid, extremeness, local density)

**Construction.** Fix a scan order and a depth d. Encode each live partial board as the one-hot vector of its (cell, tile, rotation) choices on cells 1 to d. Estimate the level's centroid from a few thousand Knuth probes, importance-reweighted (the marginal share of each choice per cell). For a node v, take three scores. Centrality is the sum over cells of the share of live nodes that make v's choice there. PC-extremeness is the norm of v's projection on the top five principal axes of the sample. Density is the number of sampled nodes that differ from v in one cell, which needs a hashed sample and is only estimable on a sample dense enough to contain Hamming neighbours. Use the score as a pruning tilt as in D4: keep the top fraction q of children by score at a chosen depth, over K rerandomised runs.

**What must be true for 10 bits.** D4's second analysis formula gives about [delta z_K + delta^2 / 2] / ln 2 bits per independent use, with delta the shift in standard deviations. At delta = 1.4 and modest K that is 1 to 3 bits, and correlation across runs or depths erodes it. Ten bits needs either a shift of about 3 sd at one depth (AUC near 0.98) or about 1.4 sd at four or more independent depths. Independence across depths is the hard part, because the same prefix is scored at each depth.

**Smallest experiment.** Generated boards 6x6 (cf 4, ci 5) and 7x7 (cf 4, ci 7), ten seeds each, all solutions enumerated. Depths at a quarter, a third, the peak and two thirds. Report AUC and top-10 percent enrichment of solution prefixes per score, with the per-board AUC as the unit of replication. Control: the same statistics computed against a random relabelling of which nodes are prefixes, matched in count. Kill line: mean AUC below 0.65 at the peak for every score, or an AUC that falls with board size from 6x6 to 7x7. Cost: about 1 core-hour in Python.

**Probe run tonight (measured, toys).** Five boards, 5x5 and 6x6, in section 3. Mean AUC at the peak is 0.50 for centrality, 0.46 for density and 0.53 for PC-extremeness. No score stays on one side of 0.5 across boards. The few values near 0.65 to 0.7 come from boards with 4 to 14 solutions, where the solutions come in small families at distance 4 to 12 and their prefixes are each other's Hamming neighbours. With about one solution on the real puzzle there is no family to find. Verdict: no signal; the kill line is met on the boards run, with the 7x7 rung not run.

### Idea B. Lifting the Birkhoff point until it sees each-tile-once (SDP rung above probe B)

**Construction.** Probe B is the first rung: graduated assignment on the rotation-lifted Birkhoff polytope (cells and tiles sum to 1, rim-consistent placements only). The energy is the expected number of matched inner edges, the schedule is beta from 0.5 upward by a factor of 1.15, and rounding is by Hungarian assignment. The second rung is a semidefinite relaxation in the ZKRW style for quadratic assignment. Y is a PSD matrix over placements p, q with Y[p,p] = x[p]; Y[p,q] = 0 when p and q use the same tile or the same cell (the "gangster" constraints, which are exactly each-tile-once and one-tile-per-cell at second order); row sums are consistent with the assignment; and the trace inner product with the matching matrix is maximised. Rounding takes the leading eigenvector, or Goemans-Williamson-style random hyperplanes onto the assignment.

**What must be true for 10 bits.** The SDP optimum must put more mass on solution placements than on placements of near-solutions far from every solution. In probe B's terms, z_vs_near must be clearly positive, and the bits it concentrates on the solution must scale with board size, so that on the 16x16 it fixes at least 10 bits that row scan cannot get from local matching. Equivalently, the gangster constraints must carry information that arc consistency does not. Gap 6a's evidence (degree-2 linearisation adds nothing beyond propagation on 4x4 and 5x5) says they do not, but that was over linear algebra, not PSD.

**Smallest experiment.** 5x5 (cf 3, ci 4) and 6x6 (cf 4, ci 5), three seeds each, all solutions enumerated, near-solution controls from probe B's annealer. Solve the SDP with a first-order solver (SCS through cvxpy, a P by P matrix with P = 478 at 5x5, 1,290 at 6x6 and 2,910 at 7x7, the rim-consistent placement counts measured by probe B). Measure z_vs_near on the diagonal x and on the leading eigenvector. Kill line: z_vs_near below 1 on every board, or bits on the solution no larger than on near-solutions. Cost: 1 to 3 core-hours, mostly the 6x6 SDP. Size wall: at 16x16 the lift has about 156,800 placements (2^17.3), so the matrix has about 2^34.5 entries (derived), beyond any solver. A positive would therefore need a decomposition argument before it bears on the real puzzle, and that halves my odds.

**Probe run tonight on the first rung (measured, toys).** Results are in section 3. The flow never produced a solution in any run, and its rounded vertex is as far from the nearest solution as from a random board. Its soft point carries at most a 2 sd tilt over typical near-solutions, at 5x5 only and with a confound. The first rung is dead as a solver. The second rung must beat z_vs_near of about 2 by a wide margin, against both kinds of control, before it means anything. The first thing to run is rung one again with mismatch-matched controls at 6x6 (a faster generator is needed), because if the 2 sd does not survive there, rung two has nothing to amplify.

### Idea C. Topology of the near-solution level sets (a mountain pass, or a proof of isolation)

**Construction.** On a board small enough to enumerate every complete board with at most k mismatches (4x4 exactly; 5x5 by DFS that tolerates up to k mismatches), build the graph G_k: vertices are those boards; edges are a swap of two same-kind tiles, or a rotation, that keeps both boards in the set. Compute the connected components of G_k for k = 0, 1, 2, and so on, and the persistence diagram of the filtration by k (H0 only: components merging). The quantity of interest is k*, the smallest k at which a solution's component contains at least half of all boards with k* mismatches. A second quantity is the barrier height: the minimum over paths from a typical best non-solution to a solution of the maximum mismatch count along the path.

**What must be true for 10 bits.** The result is meant to be a negative of proof grade: on toys of growing size, k* grows like the number of cells, so no bounded-mismatch local method reaches the solution. A positive would be k* staying small (2 to 4) as n grows, which would contradict path 4's empty band and reopen local search. The bits would then come from a walk that is polynomial inside a connected level set. I put the odds of that under 0.3 percent, since path 4's model and A10 (barrier at 5x5) both point the other way.

**Smallest experiment.** 4x4 with all boards enumerated (4 corners, 8 edges and 4 interior tiles with rotations: 3! times 8! times 4! times 4^4, about 2^30.5 boards with the pin, derived; feasible in C, borderline in Python), cf 2 or 3 and ci 2 to 4. Then 5x5 at k up to 4 by mismatch-tolerant DFS. Measure k* and the barrier height for each solution on ten seeds per size. Kill line, as a method: k* increases with n from 4x4 to 5x5. Cost: half a day of code, 2 core-hours.

## 3. The probes (toys)

### Probe A: the literal picture

Script `probe_a_projection.py` (numpy, matplotlib), outputs `out_a_*.json` and `out_a_*.png`.

What it does. Every solution of a generated board is enumerated by row-scan DFS. The solutions become points of {0,1}^(cells x tiles x 4), and pairwise distances, backbone, components and PCA are computed. Then, at four depths of the same tree, every live partial board becomes a point; it is labelled by whether a solution lies below it, and is scored by centrality, PC-extremeness and one-cell density.

What I see, plainly. The PCA of the peak level is a handful of separate blobs. ### Probe B: a flow inside the Birkhoff polytope

Script `probe_b_birkhoff_flow.py` (numpy, scipy), output `out_b.jsonl`.

What it does. Graduated assignment as in idea B, rung one, four to ten runs per board with small random perturbations of the centre point. At each step of the schedule it records, for the current point X, the bits it puts on a board s relative to the polytope centre X0: sum over cells of log2(X[c, s_c] / X0[c, s_c]), averaged over three populations. These are the solutions; 200 random rim-consistent boards; and annealed near-solutions with at most 2 mismatched edges and at least 8 cells from every solution, the null that matters. At the end it rounds X to a board.

Measured, medians over runs. The row reported is the temperature at which the solutions stand out most from random boards. Near-solutions were generated two ways. "Annealed" means a simulated-annealing run on the same mismatch count, keeping boards with 2 or fewer mismatches at 8 or more cells from every solution. "Sampled" means a randomised row-scan DFS that tolerates 2 mismatches, one board per restart, with the same filter. At 6x6 and 7x7 neither generator found a single control inside its budget (150 anneals; 3 million DFS nodes), so the near-solution comparison exists only at 5x5.

What it shows. Three things, the third of which I did not expect.

First, the flow never solves. It freezes onto a vertex that matches 82 to 92 percent of the edges and sits as far from the nearest solution as from the nearest random board (17 of 25, 29 to 30 of 36, 46 of 49 cells). As a solver it is a local search in disguise and lands where that priced-out family lands.

Second, in its soft regime (beta about 0.6 to 1.5, entropy still 3.6 to 5.1 bits per cell) the point puts 4 to 20 bits more on the solutions than its centre does, and 3 to 5 sd more than on random boards. Most of that is "edges match", which any arc-consistent search already uses.

Third, the controls disagree. Against near-solutions found by annealing the solutions have no edge (z from -0.8 to +0.2). Against near-solutions sampled by DFS they have about 2 sd (z from +1.9 to +2.4). My reading, guessed and not tested: annealing optimises the same matching energy as the flow, so it finds the boards the flow already likes, while DFS samples are closer to typical near-solutions. A residual tilt of about 2 sd toward solutions over typical near-solutions is about the size of D4's census tilt, which the second analysis formula prices at about 1 bit, and nothing says it survives at 6x6 and up, where no control could be generated. There is also a confound I did not remove: the sampled controls' two mismatched cells may simply be cells the flow disfavours, which would produce the gap with no extra information. Verdict on rung one: dead as a solver; at most a D4-sized tilt as a statistic, unproven.

## 4. Where this leaves the lens

Derived from the two probes and the list. A continuous point computed without search is a function of the local matching structure. On these toys local matching separates solutions from near-solutions far from them by 2 sd at most, and only against one of two kinds of control. That is O3 restated in geometric language, and it is why ideas 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 10, 13, 14, 16 and 18 fail for the same reason. The geometric objects that could escape it are those that encode each-tile-once at second order or higher (idea 2 and its SDP rung) or that study the level sets globally rather than through a point (idea 5). The first runs into a 2^34.5-entry wall on the real board, and the second most likely proves isolation, which is a negative. On the question, "visualise a projection of a solution through space": done on toys with every solution known, and the projection shows the tree's first branchings and nothing about where the solutions are.

Odds (guessed): idea A 0.3 percent for 10 bits; idea B 0.5 percent at toy scale, under 0.1 percent of bearing on the 16x16 without a decomposition nobody has; idea C 0.3 percent as a method, about 60 percent of producing a clean isolation negative worth a paragraph in Paper 2.

## Appendix: how to rerun, and what the runs cost

All runs: 1 core under `nice -n 10`, each under `timeout 900`. One exception, stated plainly: for a few minutes two probe processes overlapped (probe A on 6x6 seed 1 and probe B on 6x6 with looser controls), so two cores were used for that stretch. Total compute was about 25 core-minutes.

- `common.py`: the painter (copied from gap 6a and 6b, so the boards are shared with those studies), the pin, and an all-solutions row-scan DFS.
- `scan.py n cf,ci ...`: solution counts and DFS nodes used to pick boards (`scan7.out`).
- `probe_a_projection.py n cf ci seed out`: probe A; writes `out_a_*.json` and `out_a_*.png` (the projection pictures).
- `probe_b_birkhoff_flow.py n cf ci seed runs [traj] [max_mismatch] [anneal_steps] [tries] [anneal|dfs]`: probe B; `out_b.jsonl` uses annealed controls, `out_b_dfsnear.jsonl` uses DFS-sampled controls.
- `summarise.py`: prints both tables (`summary.txt`).

Run with `uv run --with numpy --with scipy --with matplotlib python -I <script> ...` from that folder.
