## 0. Result first

1. The field has no method that changes the exponent on this instance. Every published SAT, CP or MIP attack on edge matching stops at 7x7 to 8x8 on near-critical instances (2008 solvers), with 14x14 reached only on under-constrained instances with 3 or 4 interior colours [C]. No later paper moves that line; the 2018 to 2024 XCSP competitions carry an "Eternity" series but only derived boards of size 4 to 15, decision only [C].
2. Clause learning is exactly resolution with a search heuristic [C]. Resolution lower bounds for pigeonhole and matching principles are asymptotic statements about formulas that are unsatisfiable by counting; at 196 interior cells they give no number, and the refutation of a wrong border is not counting-hard in that sense [D]. The matching theorem that does bite is a different one: no polynomial CNF encoding lets unit propagation do what the all-different propagator does [C], so a CDCL refuter is strictly weaker per node than a CP solver with Régin's filter, and the repo has already measured that filter as worth under 1% of nodes [R].
3. Cube and conquer was never applied to this puzzle or to edge matching in any citable source. The record cube-and-conquer proofs cost 2^46 to 2^55 solver steps [C, D], against 2^134.4 here; scale was never the missing ingredient.
4. Prediction for the D1 measurement, stated before its result (section 3.4): no 100x wall-time win for the clause-learning refuter on frames that pass the corner blocks; b + log2 C within a few bits of the row-scan price or above it; core cell counts will look smaller than the information they carry, because 60 border pieces on 60 cells is a bijection.

## 1. The analog

| This puzzle | Object in the field | Sense of the mapping |
|---|---|---|
| 256 cells, each to receive one (piece, rotation) | CSP with 256 variables of domain up to 784, or a 0/1 placement matrix x[cell, piece, rotation] | Standard "primal" model (Ansótegui et al. 2008; Heule 2008) [C] |
| Each piece used once | one `allDifferent` over 256 variables (CP), or the functional and onto pigeonhole axioms (7.7a to 7.7d) on a 256 by 256 pigeon/hole matrix (proof complexity) | The piece/cell relation is exactly the "onto functional pigeonhole" clause set with m = n, which is satisfiable; the edge constraints make it unsatisfiable or not [D] |
| 480 edge matches | binary table constraints on grid-adjacent variables; with seam-colour variables ("dual", "diamond" variables) they become channelling clauses | Heule's compact encoding and the primal-dual encoding of Ansótegui et al. [C] |
| Grid | constraint graph of pathwidth 16 once the all-different is removed | Without distinctness the problem is a width-16 transfer matrix; the all-different makes the primal graph a clique [D] |
| Near-critical colour count | phase-transition instances of a random CSP model; Ansótegui et al. place the hardest instances where the first moment E[X] is near 1 and note that n = 16, 5 frame colours gives 17 interior colours | [C], and it is the same first-moment criterion the program uses [R] |
| Planted solution | "forced satisfiable" generators: quasigroup with holes (QWH) | QWH is the closest classical benchmark: a Latin square is planted, holes are punched, hardness peaks at a hole ratio, all-different constraints carry the structure (Achlioptas, Gomes, Kautz, Selman 2000) [C] |
| Refuting a wrong border | an UNSAT instance: interior of 196 cells with 56 boundary colours fixed | The object proof complexity talks about. Its closest named relatives: perfect matching principles (bipartite piece/cell graph), and tiling formulas such as the mutilated chessboard (perfect matching on a grid graph) [C for the relatives, D for the mapping] |

Where the analogy breaks.

- QWH has 2n small all-different constraints of arity n and binary symmetry in abundance; this puzzle has one all-different of arity 256 (or 196 plus 56 plus 4) and no symmetry at all beyond the four board rotations, which the clues remove [D].
- Pigeonhole, matching and mutilated-chessboard formulas are unsatisfiable by a counting argument and are highly symmetric. A wrong-border interior has exactly as many pieces as cells; it is unsatisfiable only because of the interaction of colours with distinctness, with no counting certificate known [D].
- Random CSP theorems assume a sparse random constraint hypergraph (an expander), domain size O(1), and n to infinity. Here the binary constraints form a grid, which does not expand (a region of k cells has a boundary of order sqrt(k)), the domain is up to 784, and n is fixed [D].
- The planted solution is irrelevant to an UNSAT sub-question (wrong border), and for the SAT question it only guarantees one model; the SAT literature on planted instances (QWH) studies finding a model, not proving absence.

### 3.2 Derivations for the wrong-border question [D throughout]

Setting: a full wrong frame is fixed; the formula Phi is the 14x14 interior, 196 pieces in 4 rotations, 56 inward colours fixed on its rim, 364 internal seams. Numbers use a uniform 17-colour model with no clue (the real colour counts are not uniform and four clues sit in the interior; the direction of each statement does not depend on that, the digits do).

D1. **Every refutation must use distinctness, heavily.** Drop the piece-used-once clauses. The remaining colour-only formula has an expected 784^196 / 17^420 = 2^167.7 models. With distinctness the expectation is 196! 4^196 / 17^420 = 2^-109.9. So distinctness removes 278 bits, and a wrong frame is unsatisfiable only through it. Check against the repo: 2^124.9 frames times 2^-109.9 is 2^15; the independently re-checked first-moment count with no clue is 2^23.5 (the gap is the non-uniform colour counts). A SAT refutation that never resolves on a piece-uniqueness clause is impossible.

D2. **But the distinctness it uses is not "counting" in the proof-complexity sense.** A dead node in the search dies in one of two ways: a cell has no candidate left (each missing candidate is either colour-incompatible or already used, and "already used" is a single binary at-most-one clause), or a group of k cells has fewer than k candidate pieces between them (a Hall violation, a pigeonhole on k). Only the second kind is hard for resolution, at cost up to 2^Theta(k) per occurrence, and easy for cutting planes and for Régin's filter. The repo has measured how often the second kind occurs beyond what pairwise reasoning sees: the matching check removes under 1% of nodes on the strong baseline (Section 1, "Hall matching", Track 4), GAC all-different leaves the 60 s score unchanged (Section 1, "Arc consistency and Régin all-different"), and Ansótegui et al.'s CTadiff column moves medians by a factor 0.78 to 1.15 [C]. Reason: far from the frontier every cell still has all unused pieces as candidates, so Hall sets can only form among frontier cells with tiny domains, where k is 2 to 4. Consequence: the exponential separations (resolution vs cutting planes, CNF vs propagator) are real and their domain is nearly empty on this instance. Pseudo-Boolean or cardinality reasoning is predicted to buy well under one bit.

D3. **What the matching lower bounds say numerically: nothing at this size.** Razborov's dense-graph bound is quoted in follow-up papers as exp(Omega(delta / log^2 n)) (I did not open the original; section 5). With n = 196 and even delta = 196, delta / log2(n)^2 = 3.4, a constant swallowed by the Omega. The 2^Omega(n) bounds for constant-degree graphs have unknown constants. No asymptotic lower bound in the field certifies that a 196-cell refutation is long. The price of refuting a wrong frame is an empirical quantity.

D4. **The refutation is global, not local.** Each interior cell placed against two fixed neighbours has 784 (1 - x) / 289 = 2.71 (1 - x) candidates, x being the share of pieces used; this exceeds 1 until 63% of the interior is filled. So no block or band of the interior that touches the rim on fewer than all sides is over-constrained in expectation: a k x k corner block has about 2.71^(k^2) fillings before depletion bites, the first ring alone has about 2^47.8 fillings for a given frame. Under the same model the frame-given interior tree peaks at 2^74 (row scan), 2^68 (inward spiral), with no clue; the five clues are worth 32 bits at the full row-scan peak (2^166.2 with none, 2^134.4 with five, ROUTES Section 1), so subtracting them gives about 2^36 to 2^42 for the frame-given tree with clues, against the repo's argued 10^8.3 = 2^27.6 (ROUTES Section 3, "Frame-first enumeration"). The subtraction is crude (the clues need not be worth the same at a different peak) and I do not claim a contradiction; D1 stage 0 measures the quantity directly. What the argument does establish: outside the clue windows there is no small unsatisfiable sub-region to find, so a cheap core must draw on the whole rim.

D5. **Upper bounds on proof size, and where learning could beat a tree.** Tree-like resolution of Phi is at most the DFS tree in the best order. General resolution can be smaller only by reusing a lemma on many branches. A learned clause here mentions seam colours along the current frontier and the pieces whose unavailability was used. It fires again only when another branch reproduces those colours with those pieces still unavailable. Frontier patterns live in a space of 17^14 = 2^57 against a tree of 2^28 to 2^42 nodes, so full-frontier recurrence is negligible; partial recurrence (a pocket of two to four seams) is what the repo measured as "boundary keys recur 46% but only 13.5% soundly reusable" at 7x7 (Section 1, "Small-region reasoning"). Prediction: the conflict count of a clause-learning solver is within a small factor of the node count of a good DFS, not exponentially below it. This is also what the 2008 data show at 7x7 and 8x8 [C].

D6. **No symmetry, so nothing for symmetry breaking, PR clauses or extended resolution to grab.** The mutilated-chessboard short proofs work because two placements covering the same squares are interchangeable. The analog here is two fillings of a region with the same pieces and the same boundary colours. For a 2 x 2 block the expected number of such alternatives is 4! 4^4 / 17^12, about 10^-11. The piece set was built without duplicates (ROUTES Section 6: 114 "near-twins" are an orientation artefact). The global symmetry group is the four rotations, removed by the clues: at most 2 bits.

D7. **Width.** Without distinctness, resolution width is bounded by the interior pathwidth: about 14 seams times log2(17) = 57 bits of state, proofs of size at most about 2^57 times a polynomial, and the formula is satisfiable anyway (D1). With distinctness, a clause that summarises a region must name the pieces used inside it: width up to 196. This is the proof-complexity form of the plan's sentence "any transfer-matrix state must carry the set of used tiles" (ROUTES Section 4, "Tensor networks ... transfer matrix with used set").

### 3.3 What the field says about clause learning on near-critical random structured instances

- [C] It helps when a short DAG-shaped resolution proof exists and the heuristics can find it (crafted families; Elffers et al. 2018). It does not help on unstructured random formulas, where CDCL is "noticeably hard" at 350 variables and lookahead (no learning) is the method of choice (handbook footnote 18; Heule et al. 2011).
- [C] On edge matching itself the only controlled comparison is from 2008: learning is roughly level with a tuned arc-consistency solver at 6x6 and 7x7 and 5x to 10x behind at 8x8, "with a poor scaling behaviour". Heule's finding that PicoSAT needs the redundant at-most-one-square-per-piece clauses to solve anything is the same fact as D1: without explicit distinctness the solver is lost.
- [D] Reading: on this structure learning is a constant-factor matter of either sign, drifting negative with size. No paper measures the exponent of a clause-learning solver against plain DFS on edge matching above 8x8. That measurement does not exist in the literature; the program's "CP-SAT pushes the planted wall about one size past a restarting DFS" (literature memo) is the only post-2013 data point I know of, and it is the repo's own.

### 3.4 Predictions for Track D1, written before its report [D]

Population: wrong frames that pass the four clue corner blocks (the 2026-11-13 memo's correction; on uniform frames the solver will find the 1 to 4 cell corner cores and look excellent for a reason that teaches nothing).

- P1. Stage 1 kill line ("no refuter 100 times cheaper in wall time on the median frame") is hit: the clause-learning solver is not 100x cheaper than plain search. Odds 85%. Basis: D5 plus a per-conflict cost 10^3 to 10^4 times a DFS node.
- P2. Conflicts needed by the SAT solver are within 2^5 either way of the nodes of the best plain search on the same frame. Odds 70%.
- P3. Stage 2: median b + log2 C on this population lands at 130 bits or above, that is, at the row-scan price or worse once C is converted to node-equivalents. Odds 80%. The pass line (below 120 with an interval excluding 134.4) is met with odds 3%.
- P4. Core cell counts will understate the information in the core. 60 pieces on 60 cells is a bijection, so the cells left out of a core are not free: deleting one border assumption leaves one unused piece for one cell, and the solver re-derives it by unit propagation. A freed contiguous arc of m cells holding exactly m free pieces has about m! / 5^(m+1) valid arrangements: less than 1 up to m = 12, 1 at m = 13, 2^12 at m = 20, 2^36 at m = 30 (stdlib). Scattered freed cells are worth far less: 30 cells in 10 arcs carry 2^15. So a deletion-minimal core of 30 to 45 cells can still pin the frame to within a handful of alternatives, and b counted as volume stays within 15 to 35 bits of its full-frame value. The preregistered alternative kill ("median core of 54 cells or more") can therefore be missed for free; only b by volume, with the freed cells in long arcs, means anything. Odds that D1 reports cores under 54 cells while b by volume stays above 90 bits (in the uniform count): 60%.
- P5. If pseudo-Boolean or matching-augmented refuters are in the comparison, they tie the clausal one within a factor 2 in conflicts or nodes. Odds 85% (D2).
- What would surprise me and reopen the route: conflicts below nodes by 2^10 or more on block-passing frames, with cores whose freed cells sit in one arc of 20 or more. That would mean short DAG proofs exist, against D5.

## 4. Transfer candidates

Nothing in this field transfers as a route to a solution. The three items below are stated so they can be killed; the first two are expected to die and the third is an instrument.

### T1. Clause-learning refuter with explained propagation as the engine of the frame search

- On this puzzle: primal-dual encoding (placement plus seam-colour variables), piece uniqueness explicit, lazy explanations for the all-different, partial frames passed as assumptions, cores stored as nogoods. This is D1's design with a lazy-clause-generation refuter.
- Cost: price = 134.4 - g + o bits, with g = log2(DFS nodes / solver conflicts) on the arcs that carry the price and o = log2(cost of a conflict / cost of a DFS node). o = log2(10^8 / 10^5) to log2(10^8 / 10^4) = 10.0 to 13.3 bits (10^8 nodes per core-second is the repo's rate; the conflict rate is a guess). g: the literature gives no node ratio; the 2008 time ratios (0.6x to 2.8x at 6x6 and 7x7, 5x to 10x at 8x8, against CP solvers far slower than 10^8 nodes per second) suggest g of 0 to 5 bits (a guess). Price: 134.4 - (0 to 5) + (10 to 13) = 139 to 148 bits, a loss of 5 to 13 bits. Break-even needs g of 10 to 13 bits; reaching 1,000 core-years (72.9 bits below the price) needs g of 83 to 86 bits.
- Cheapest kill: D1 itself. If not decisive: on generated critical boards n = 6 to 9, refute wrong frames with CaDiCaL (primal-dual) and with row-major DFS, and fit log2(nodes / conflicts) against n. A slope at or below zero kills it. Under one core-hour at these sizes.
- Odds: g of 13 bits or more at 16x16, 2%. A change of exponent, under 0.5%.
- ROUTES: Section 3, "Cheap refutation of wrong frames (Track D1)" and "Nogood-driven frame search"; Section 4, "SAT / CSP encodings, cube and conquer".

### T2. Cardinality (cutting-planes) conflict analysis instead of resolution

- On this puzzle: pass exactly-one-per-cell and at-most-one-per-piece as native cardinality constraints to a pseudo-Boolean solver (RoundingSat class), so that Hall-type conflicts are learned as one inequality.
- Cost: gain is bounded by the share of the proof spent on Hall violations that pairwise reasoning misses. The repo's measured share is under 1% of nodes at 6x6 to 8x8 (Track 4), so gain is about -log2(0.99) = 0.015 bits if that share transfers to 16x16 (the transfer is the guess), against a per-conflict cost at least that of CDCL. Price: 134.4 + 10 or more.
- Cheapest kill: run a PB solver and CaDiCaL on the same D1 frames (or on the committed halo UNSAT instances). A conflict ratio under 2 kills it. Minutes.
- Odds of 3 bits or more: 2%.
- ROUTES: no row (the literature memo lists it as untested); Section 1, "Hall matching", is the measured ceiling.

### T3. Proof logging and cube-and-conquer as instruments for the program's UNSAT claims

- On this puzzle: emit DRAT or VeriPB certificates for the halo rigidity results, for "no 471 inside this neighbourhood", for sub-board UNSAT, and for D1's cores; split long UNSAT runs by cubes so they resume and parallelise. Checking costs about as much as solving (16,000 against 13,000 CPU hours in the Pythagorean proof) [C].
- Cost toward a solution: 0 bits. Capacity at record scale is 2^47 to 2^55 steps, 79 to 87 bits short of 2^134.4.
- Cheapest kill: none needed; it is not a route. Its value is that "proven" on the site would mean "checked certificate". Half a day for the first certificate on a committed halo instance.
- Odds it is useful as an instrument: 60%. Odds it bears on a full solution: none.
- ROUTES: "Looks untried" (proof logging); Section 5, "A 470 board as a damaged solution" (flagged disagreement 9 concerns an uncommitted MIP table that a certificate would settle).
