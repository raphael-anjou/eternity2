## 0. Answer in ten lines

1. **No published or reported method has solved an edge-matching instance at the critical colour density beyond 10x10 without extra pinned tiles.** Every large solved instance I found is off the critical point in one of four ways: far fewer colours (Heule 2009: 11x11, 12x12, 14x14 with 3 or 4 centre colours, solved in seconds), more colours (community 16x16 with 23 to 24 colour types, full search in minutes), many extra hints (a 16x16 E2-like benchmark with 20 hints, fully searched in 2.9e13 nodes, digest), or freedom over which pieces to use (14x14 from any 196 of the 256 pieces; 16x16 minus a 6x6 hole, digest). At the peak, 2008 SAT and CSP solvers stopped at 8x8 (Ansótegui et al., opened), and the community's largest exhaustive solves are 9x9 and 10x10 benchmarks by brute force.
2. **How Eternity I fell, from the primary interview:** Selby and Riordan saw that difficulty peaks where the expected number of solutions is 1 (about 70 pieces for that puzzle) and that the 209-piece puzzle sat far above it (about 10^95 solutions). They fitted per-piece "goodness" by Bayesian models of exhaustive tilings of small regions by random piece subsets, placed bad pieces first, and finished by a beam search. The lever was surplus: surplus solutions, and in the endgame a surplus of pieces to choose from.
3. **Eternity II removes the surplus, and the program's own numbers show it exactly.** Expected nodes per solution under row scan are 10^42.97 with no clue and 10^42.97 with the starter only (from ROUTES 10^50.04 / 1.153e7 and 10^47.14 / 14,702), against 10^40.46 for the whole five-clue tree. Relaxing toward more solutions makes the search more expensive per solution; the only relaxation with real surplus is the partial-match goal, which is what record engines already exploit.
4. **Computer-vision jigsaw solvers run entirely on a signal Eternity II does not have**: a continuous pairwise dissimilarity whose best match is usually unique ("best buddies", ratio-to-second-best weights). With 17 colours each half-edge has about 48 equally perfect partners. Demaine and Demaine state the dividing line: if locally fitting pairs are globally correct, joining pairs solves the puzzle in polynomial time; ambiguity is what makes it hard.
5. **Strongest barrier-type result:** the only polynomial-time assembly theorem for random edge-matching (Bordenave, Feige, Mossel 2016, Theorem 1.1) needs q >= n^(1+eps) colours and windows of side 2k+1 with k = 200/eps. At q = 17, n = 16, eps = 0.022 and k is about 9,100: local windows identify nothing on this board, which is the program's own finding ("local reasoning sees nothing beyond the corners") stated as a theorem's failure condition. It is an upper-bound method failing, not a lower bound.
6. **Nothing in this field transfers with credible odds.** One candidate (piece goodness as a value order) is priced at a guessed 6 bits against the 56 to 66 needed; one instrument (the hint exchange rate) is worth running for calibration; the Selby endgame transfer is priced out by 13 orders.

## 1. The analog

**Exact analog: the bounded unsigned edge-matching puzzle with rotation**, which is the Wang tiling problem restricted to a finite square, with each tile used exactly once and tiles allowed to rotate. Demaine and Demaine (2007) prove it NP-complete (Theorem 3, quoted in section 2). Wang tiles proper differ in two ways that matter: tiles do not rotate and each tile type may be used any number of times. Unlimited reuse is exactly what makes the transfer-matrix and frontier methods work for Wang and polyomino tilings, and exactly what Eternity II forbids (section 2, Kawahara et al.).

What maps to what:

| Eternity II | Tiling and jigsaw literature |
|---|---|
| 256 distinct pieces, rotations allowed | "Two-set GEMP-F" (Ansótegui et al.), "bounded unsigned edge matching" (Heule), random jigsaw with q jig types (Mossel and Ross; Bordenave, Feige, Mossel), apictorial edge-matching (Kovalsky et al.) |
| all-different over 256 pieces | each piece used once; in exact cover (DLX, XCC) the primary column of each piece |
| planted, unpublished solution | the "planted assembly" of the random jigsaw papers; the original image in computer-vision jigsaws |
| 22 colours, 5 frame and 17 interior, chosen near E[Z] = 1 | q colours; the uniqueness threshold q about (2/sqrt(e)) n (Martinsson, in LIT 5.1); Selby and Riordan's "critical size" where expected solutions equal 1 |
| five clues | Tomy's four clue puzzles and the starter piece |

**Where the analogy breaks.**
- Random jigsaw models colour every edge independently and uniformly, have no frame class and usually no rotation (Bordenave, Feige, Mossel label each half-edge by orientation). Eternity II has exact colour multiplicities, a two-sided frame ring and rotations. The theorems therefore give the shape of the phenomenon, not numbers for this board.
- Computer-vision jigsaws have continuous, highly informative pairwise costs and grade success by the percentage of correct neighbours (about 95 percent on 540 to 3,300 pieces in Yu, Russell, Agapito, Table 1). Eternity II has binary compatibility and demands 100 percent.
- Eternity I is a polydrafter packing (209 pieces on a dodecagon), not edge matching. What transfers is the counting argument and the "goodness" method, not the geometry.

## 3. Hardness evidence

1. **Worst-case NP-completeness** (Demaine and Demaine 2007, Theorem 3; theorem). Assumptions: a family of instances of growing size. It says nothing about one fixed 16x16 instance and is not a barrier here.
2. **Ambiguity is necessary for hardness** (same paper, section 1). The converse direction is what matters: Eternity II has maximal ambiguity per edge (each interior half-edge has about 48 perfect partners among the other pieces' rotated sides, since the 420 interior-colour joins give 840 half-edges over 17 colours, 49.4 per colour on average; the counts are near flat per Hopfer's balance argument, `digests/0007`, digest). Every method whose engine is a unique or ratio-dominant best match (all CV jigsaw solvers above, and Sholomon's best-buddy phase) has no input on this instance. This is a structural barrier for a whole class, valid for this instance.
3. **Local windows fail below n^(1+eps)** (Bordenave, Feige, Mossel 2016, Theorem 1.1 and section 5; theorem for the upper bound only). The theorem guarantees local identifiability for windows of radius k = 200/eps. For this board eps = ln(17/16)/ln(16) = 0.0219 and k = 9,147, far beyond the board. It is not a lower bound (smaller windows might work), but together with Martinsson's non-uniqueness for q <= (2/sqrt(e)) n (LIT 5.1; 2/sqrt(e) x 16 = 19.4 > 17) it places the interior on the side where no local certificate is known to exist. Assumptions that fail here: uniform independent colours, no rotation, n to infinity. The program's measured version is ROUTES 3: about 20 cheap bits, all at the four corners.
4. **Every fill order is exponential when solutions are many, and the frame-first order is the worst in the unique regime** (Edmonds, Edmonds, Mertz; LIT 5.2, independently re-checked with a correction in `literature-and-path-6-recheck.md` L5). Already examined; not repeated.
5. **Empirical phase transition at the hardness peak**: Ansótegui et al. 2008 (opened) locate the peak at "cm = 17" interior colours for n = 16 and cf = 5 (`M13/how-the-puzzle-was-made.md` row 7) and report that 2008 SAT and CSP solvers stop at 8x8 there. Experiment, old solvers.
6. **The surplus argument, quantified on this instance** (arithmetic on ROUTES numbers, independently re-checked inputs). Selby and Riordan's method works when expected solutions are astronomically many. Here, nodes per solution under row scan: 10^50.04 / 1.153e7 = 10^42.98 with no clue; 10^47.14 / 14,702 = 10^42.97 with the starter. The full five-clue tree is 10^40.46. So moving toward more solutions buys nothing per solution, and adding clues buys 2.5 decades. McGavin observed the same invariance of nodes per solution in 2011 (`digests/0013` and PAGE_CANDIDATES, msg 8924; digest). This is the precise sense in which the designers "removed the weakness": with 22 colours on 256 pieces there is no surplus at any clue level. The assumption (solutions spread uniformly through the tree) is the first-moment model Track 10 validated to 0.03 digits.

No result in this field gives a lower bound for complete search on a fixed planted instance at the threshold.

## 4. Transfer candidates

Baseline: 134.4 bits (10^40.46 nodes, five clues, row scan). Gap to 1,000 core-years: 17 to 20 orders, i.e. 56.5 to 66.4 bits (ROUTES 1).

### Transfer 1: Selby and Riordan goodness as a value order in strict search

- **Statement.** Fit per-piece (and per-piece-pair) goodness from exhaustive tilings of small regions by random piece subsets, as Selby and Riordan did and as Verhaard did for 2x3 frame regions, and use it to order candidate values in strict five-clue search (bad pieces tried first where they fit).
- **Cost in bits.** Value order does not change the size of a complete tree; it changes time to the first solution. With about one solution, gain = sum over the depths where order matters of log2(1 / (2(1 - a))), where a is the probability that the true value is ranked above a random sibling (a = 0.5 is the random-order baseline that halves the tree). Guess a = 0.6 (A16's backdoor predictor reached AUC 0.637 but only +0.010 over domain size) and D_eff = 20 effective depths (guess). Gain = 20 x log2(1/0.8) = 6.4 bits. Need 56.5. Both factors are guesses; to reach 56.5 bits at D_eff = 20 needs a = 0.93.
- **Cheapest kill.** On 30 planted 8x8 and 10x10 critical boards (ROUTES 5 growth-law ladder), fit goodness from 2x2 and 2x3 region enumerations of random subsets (no solution in hand), then measure the AUC of the true piece among the legal candidates at each depth of the solution path, against the domain-size baseline. Kill if AUC minus the baseline AUC is below 0.05. A few core-hours.
- **Already examined?** Close relatives killed: ROUTES 1 "Value order by propagation impact (A21)" and ROUTES 6 "Planted-solution recovery by spectral pair statistics (A1)". Goodness from small-region enumeration itself has no row.
- **Odds** that it changes the exponent enough to matter: under 1 percent. Reason: Track 10 shows the real tree matches a model that treats pieces as exchangeable given colour counts to 0.03 digits, which leaves little piece heterogeneity for a goodness score to find.

### Transfer 2 (instrument, not a method): the hint exchange rate

- **Statement.** The community's hint benchmarks imply a price for pinned tiles: 20 hints on an E2-like 16x16 cost 10^13.47 nodes (hints.20.3, digest), five clues cost 10^40.46 on the real set. That is (40.46 - 13.47) / 15 = 1.80 decades, about 6.0 bits, per extra correct pin, so the 56.5 to 66.4 missing bits correspond to about 9 to 11 extra correctly pinned tiles. Measuring this curve on the program's own instruments would convert any partial-information source (frame marginals, a guessed painter, a corner table) into "equivalent pins".
- **Cost in bits.** It is a yardstick, not a gain. Caveats that are guesses: hints.20.3 is Owen's E2-like set, not the real pieces; the colour counts and hint placement are unverified; "nodes" are counted by a different solver (one with domain reduction), so the 13.47 is likely low for plain row scan.
- **Cheapest experiment.** With Track 10's estimator, pin k true tiles (k = 5, 8, 11, 14, 20, scattered and clustered) on three generated 16x16 boards painted from the real colour counts and record log10 tree size against k. One core-day. It is killed as useful if the per-pin rate varies by more than a factor 2 with placement, since then "equivalent pins" is not a well-defined unit.
- **Already examined?** No row. Owen's 2007 simulation (about 20 well-placed hints needed for CPU-year range, `digests/0001`; digest) is the only earlier estimate.
- **Odds** of producing a useful calibration: about 60 percent. Odds that it leads to a solution: none by itself.

### Transfer 3 (priced out here): Selby's endgame with a surplus pool

- **Statement.** E1's endgame tiled a region using any 24 of 90 available pieces. The E2 analog that the community did solve is the 16x16 minus a 6x6 hole (36 pieces left free). The transfer would be: produce hole-solutions, then require the 36 leftover pieces to tile the hole.
- **Cost.** Hole-solution cost: about 160 core-hours per solution (20 h on 8 backtrackers, digest) at a guessed 1e8 to 1e9 nodes per second, so 10^13.8 to 10^14.8 nodes. Probability that the leftover 36 pieces tile the 6x6 hole whose 24 boundary edges are fixed: 36! x 4^36 / 17^84 (60 internal plus 24 boundary edges, 17 colours) = 10^63.24 / 10^103.36 = 10^-40.1. Total 10^53.9 to 10^54.9 nodes, 13 to 14 orders above the baseline. The pool surplus is what made the hole puzzle easy, and the closure probability takes it all back.
- **Already examined?** Same structure as ROUTES 2 "Meet in the middle, sliced seam join" and ROUTES 3 "Frame-first enumeration": a split with a fixed piece pool pays the closure probability. Not proposed.

**Not proposed, with reasons.** CV jigsaw solvers (LP, GA with agreement and best-buddy crossover, loop constraints): their input is a ranked, mostly unambiguous pairwise cost; here compatibility is binary and about 48-fold ambiguous (section 3 item 2). Agreement crossover between two high-scoring boards would also inherit adjacencies with no bearing on the solution, since path 4 places boards at 470 about 246 to 254 cells from any solution (ROUTES 5, argued, not independently checked). DLX and XCC: MRV backtracking with no change of exponent. ZDD and frontier methods: the all-different state is not frontier-local, by the method's own stated requirement. Kovalsky's LP: the 12x12 success has no stated colour count and no rotation in the main algorithm.

Three papers to read in full:
1. Selby, "Eternity: description of method" (archived archduke.org): the only full statement of the goodness model and of how the beam search and region shapes were chosen; needed before running transfer 1.
2. Bordenave, Feige, Mossel 2016 (arXiv:1605.03086), sections 2 and 5: whether the constant c = 200 is an artefact of the proof, i.e. whether windows of practical size could identify neighbourhoods at q slightly above n with rotations and a frame.
3. Benoist and Bourreau 2008, "Fast global filtering for Eternity II": the only published global-filtering paper on the instance itself, with a benchmark set the program has never run.
