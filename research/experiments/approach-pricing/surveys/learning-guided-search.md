## 1. The analog

Machine learning has no benchmark with the structure of Eternity II. The nearest ones are four, each matching one part of it:

| ML benchmark | What maps to tiles | All-different | Planted solution | Density | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random k-SAT near threshold, SR(n) (NeuroSAT, G4SATBench, GQSAT) | clauses | none | no (random), or planted variants | at the threshold, like E2's critical colour count | no global permutation constraint; instance sizes 40 to 400 variables, all solved by a stock CDCL solver in well under a second |
| Sudoku (RRN, SATNet, TRM, diffusion, Giannoulis et al.) | givens + cells | yes (rows, columns, boxes) | yes, unique by design | hard for humans, easy for propagation | 81 cells and 9 values against 256 cells and 1,024 placements per cell; and Sudoku is propagation-easy (Giannoulis et al.: almost all puzzles need at most one guess) where E2's propagation sees nothing past the corners |
| Image jigsaw reassembly (Alphazzle, ERL-MPP) | patches | yes (permutation) | yes (the photograph) | n/a | each edge pair carries a continuous similarity score from pixels; in E2 the only signal is colour equality, which a classical solver already sees in full. Sizes 3x3 to 5x5 (Alphazzle) and up to 150 pieces (ERL-MPP) |
| Planted inference: stochastic block model, planted colouring (Chen et al., Krzakala and Zdeborová) | edges of a random graph | no | yes | below or at the detection threshold | sparse random graph, n to infinity; E2 is a fixed 16x16 grid |

The precise sense of the analogy that matters: E2 is a planted random CSP at the satisfiability threshold, plus a global all-different. In ML terms the "task" is either (a) search: return the planted solution, a binary-reward decision problem, or (b) inference: predict the planted placement from the tile set. FunSearch and AlphaEvolve work on (c), extremal and optimisation problems with a graded score; the E2 analogue of (c), maximising matched edges, is the 470 regime, which the program argues is decoupled from (a) (ROUTES section 5, row "A 470 board as a damaged solution": about 10^33 boards at 470, almost all 246 to 254 cells from any solution; argued, not independently checked).

What the table says in one line: every learned method with a published gain on hard random instances works at sizes a classical solver finishes in milliseconds (SR(40) to SR(400), 3-SAT with 5 to 250 variables, 9x9 Sudoku, 5x5 jigsaw), and every published gain at competition scale (NeuroCore, NeuroBack, Graph-Q-SAT's iteration counts) is a constant factor of 5% to 20% more problems, one of which turned out to be reproducible with random scores. Program search (FunSearch, AlphaEvolve) improved only extremal or optimisation problems with a graded score. I found no case where it solved a decision problem with a single planted solution; its own authors list a binary signal as outside its scope.

## 3. Hardness evidence

### 3.1 Barriers that bound any branching heuristic, learned or evolved

1. **Resolution lower bounds for random CSPs.** Chan and Molloy, "A Dichotomy Theorem for the Resolution Complexity of Random Constraint Satisfaction Problems", FOCS 2008, version of 2012, https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~molloy/webpapers/csp11-2.pdf, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2: for random CSPs with domain size O(1), constraints on O(1) variables, cn constraints, "either the resolution complexity is almost surely polylogarithmic for sufficiently large c, or it is almost surely exponential for every c > 0". Their introduction: "virtually every complete SAT-solver or CSP-solver used in practice is resolution based. The running time of any such algorithm is lower-bounded by the resolution complexity of the input." Grade: theorem. Consequence: a learned or evolved branching order inside DFS or CDCL picks a proof inside resolution; it cannot go below the shortest refutation. Do the assumptions hold here? No, not literally: E2 has domain 1,024 per cell, a global all-different, a grid instead of a random hypergraph, and a fixed size. What carries over is the logic: the cost the program measures is dominated by refuting wrong partial frames (ROUTES section 3), which is refutation, and that is where a heuristic is bounded by a proof system rather than by cleverness.

2. **Training data for hard decision problems is biased.** Yehuda, Gabel, Schuster, ICML 2020, https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09398, Theorem 1: "If L is NP-complete, then there is no efficient complete sampler for it, unless NP = coNP"; abstract: "any polynomial-time sample generator for an NP-hard problem samples, in fact, from an easier sub-problem." Geisler et al., ICLR 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.10942, name the same "easier subproblem" and "spurious features" failure. Grade: theorem (conditional on NP ≠ coNP). Does it hold here? Half. For predicting the planted solution it does NOT bite: a planted painter samples (tile set, solution) pairs cheaply, and Track 10 found the official set ordinary among 20 boards painted from its colour counts, so the training distribution can match the target. For training a refuter or a value function on wrong frames it DOES bite: every "this frame has no completion" label is a refutation, the expensive object itself.

3. **Learned guidance tends to be classical machinery in disguise.** NeuroCore's corrected ablation (random scores equal on SATCOMP-2018) and Böther et al. ("can be replaced by random values", first-pass memo) are experiments, not theorems, but they are the two audited cases in the field and both went the same way. G4SATBench finds GNNs learn greedy local search and fail to learn backtracking. Grade: experiment.

4. **Program search needs a graded, hill-climbable score.** FunSearch (binary signal out of scope), Georgiev et al. section 1.6 (smooth score, "struggles otherwise"), Wagner (sparse reward trains slowly). Grade: authors' own experimental statement. For E2 the graded proxy is matched edges, and the program's model says high-score boards are far from solutions (ROUTES section 5, path 4, argued, not independently checked). So the score the method needs is the one the program believes is misleading; if path 4 is right, this is a barrier, not a tuning problem.

### 3.2 Scaling evidence

No learned solver of any kind has published a gain on a random or planted CSP at threshold at a size where classical exact search is the bottleneck. CLRS (four times larger graphs break MPNNs), Joshi et al. (few hundred nodes), G4SATBench (limited size generalisation) and NeuroSAT (25% at SR(200)) all degrade with size. E2 at 16x16 is about 2^134 nodes for the best exact search; the largest learned-solver instances above are solved by classical tools in milliseconds.

### 3.3 The inference view: can a learned predictor beat first-moment reasoning?

Question: on an instance whose search tree matches the random model to 0.03 digits (Track 10), is there any information-theoretic reason a learned predictor of the planted placement could do better than first-moment reasoning?

**Against (stronger, in my reading):**

- Information is not the barrier; computation is. With five clues the expected number of solutions of a random tile set is 4.1e-8 (ROUTES section 5, first-moment row), so the instance plus clues pins the planted solution: the Bayes posterior is essentially a point mass. Any predictor, learned or not, is a polynomial-time approximation of the posterior marginals P(tile t at cell c | instance). The question is purely whether a polynomial-time statistic correlates with them.
- With one clue there are about 14,702 solutions in expectation. If the planted ensemble is "quiet" there, the planted solution is statistically one of ~1.5e4 typical solutions, and the posterior marginal at a cell is a mixture over them. Krzakala and Zdeborová, PRL 102 (2009), https://arxiv.org/abs/0901.2130, p.2: where the liquid fixed point is stable, "the properties of the planted ensemble are exactly the same as the properties of the purely random ensemble, up to the existence of the planted state", and "If one does not discover the planted cluster, the planted and the purely random graphs are indistinguishable and we thus expect that in such a case they have comparable algorithmic difficulty" (heuristic claim from the cavity method, sparse random graphs, N to infinity). The program's measurements are what quiet planting predicts: the quenched node count equals the annealed first moment to 0.03 digits, arc consistency over all 196 interior cells adds nothing past the corner windows (0.4313 vs 0.4293, ROUTES section 3), and a spectral statistic ranks true neighbours below colour rarity (A1, z = -7.94 and -5.91, ROUTES section 6).
- Planted k-SAT gives an unconditional class-wide bound: Feldman, Perkins, Vempala, https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.4821 (v8 2018), abstract: a lower bound "tight up to logarithmic factors, for statistical (query) algorithms", covering approaches with "statistical analogues (spectral, MCMC, gradient-based, convex optimization etc.)". Grade: theorem, for planted k-CSPs with n to infinity and a clause count that grows. A network trained by gradient descent and then applied is in this broad family. It does not literally apply: E2 has a fixed 256 "clauses" and one instance.
- The low-degree conjecture formalises the same expectation: Kunisky, Wein, Bandeira, https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11636, Conjecture 1.16 (bounded low-degree likelihood ratio implies "no polynomial-time algorithm that strongly distinguishes" planted from null). Grade: conjecture, asymptotic, for "sufficiently nice" sequences. Recovery is at least as hard as detection. At a fixed size of 16 it predicts nothing quantitative.
- The best learned predictors on planted inference match BP and do not cross the conjectured computational threshold (Chen, Li, Bruna 2019, finite-size experiments). Learning finds the BP-class algorithm; it has not been shown to find a better one.

**For (weaker, but not zero):**

- The 0.03-digit agreement is about one statistic, the count of partial boards per depth, dominated by partial frames at the peak. It is a low-order statistic. A useful predictor needs about 56 bits (ROUTES section 1, first row) out of a configuration space of log2(4! x 56! x 196! x 4^196) ≈ 1,860 bits. Matching the aggregate count does not exclude a signal that small; quiet planting is an asymptotic statement and the grid is not a sparse random graph.
- The instance may not be a uniform planted draw. The maker's painter is unknown; the coarse-stratified-painter loophole (29 to 118 bits, ROUTES section 6, second row) is invisible in piece statistics and only testable by search under a guessed painter. A model trained on boards from a guessed painter is one way to run that test, but it is the existing row, not a new route, and its author's odds are 1 in 20,000.
- The five clues are a planted-solution leak of a few dozen bits by design (about 38 bits between the one-clue and five-clue first moments: log2(14,702 / 4.1e-8) ≈ 38.4). Exploiting them requires reasoning about consequences far from the clue, which is exactly what local reasoning was measured not to do.

Net: no information-theoretic reason forbids a learned predictor; the computational evidence (quiet-planting phenomenology, statistical-query and low-degree heuristics, and the program's own measurements) predicts it will reproduce colour-rarity and corner information and stop there. The decisive experiment is cheap and is candidate 2 below.

## 4. Transfer candidates

### 4.1 Evolved ordering code (agenda E1), and what makes a test of it trustworthy

**Statement.** An LLM-driven program search (FunSearch, AlphaEvolve, OpenEvolve) over the cell-order and value-order hooks of `bb-mrv`, scored on work, finds an order whose advantage over `bb-mrv` grows with n on sizes it never saw.

**What the literature predicts.** (1) It will find something on the calibration rungs: the score is graded (nodes or work within a budget), the hook is isolated, the evaluator is fast; these are FunSearch's three conditions, and FunSearch's online bin-packing heuristic did generalise across sizes (OR1 to OR4, Weibull 5k to 100k, gap widening with size). (2) The gain will most likely be a constant factor rather than a slope: every learned or evolved branching gain at scale in section 2 is 5% to 20% more instances or 2 to 3x fewer decisions, and NeuroCore's vanished under a random-score control. (3) The known failure modes are cheating the verifier (Georgiev et al.) and fitting the sizes it saw (CLRS, Joshi et al.).

**A static order cannot help; only a dynamic one can.** For a static order the program already has a parameter-free evaluator: the first-moment count A(S) over the chain of filled sets (growth-law memo section 2), validated on the real 16x16 for row scan to 0.03 digits. I ran it in a uniform-colour version (c = 17 interior, 5 frame, no clues; not the real colour counts, so only differences between orders mean anything). Log10 expected total nodes: row scan 51.37 (peak 50.09 at depth 161; the repo's real-count model gives 10^50.04 with no clue), spiral inward 55.84, frame first then rows 57.56, anti-diagonal 57.72. A greedy order that minimises A at each step reproduces row scan exactly, and simulated annealing over 30,000 local moves (cell moved up to 12 positions) found nothing below 51.37. So, in the model, row scan is at least a local optimum among static orders, and evolving a static order is pointless: score candidate static orders on A(S) at 16x16 directly, at no search cost. Evolution has a reason to exist only for dynamic, value-dependent orders (MRV-like), which act through propagation. The growth-law second analysis rated "order and propagation are only surface factors" as not established, and noted that no argument covers a dynamic order (growth-law-recheck.md, C1).

**Cost in bits.** Gain at 16x16 ≈ g8 + s x 8, where g8 is the measured gain at 8x8 and s is the slope in bits per unit n. Best measured propagating lead: A22, G1 ratio 0.147 at 8x8 c=10, so g8 = log2(1/0.147) = 2.8 bits. A22's exploratory median log10 ratios over n = 6..8 (-0.26, -0.38, -0.83) give s ≈ 0.285 digits = 0.95 bits per n (exploratory, three points, and the growth-law second analysis found no growth in paired medians 9.9, 15.7, 5.4). Extrapolated: 2.8 + 0.95 x 8 ≈ 10.4 bits, so cost about 134.4 - 10.4 = 124 bits. Getting to the 1,000 core-year line (56 to 66 bits) needs s ≥ (56 - 2.8)/8 ≈ 6.7 bits per unit n, seven times the best exploratory slope. The factor s for an evolved order is a pure guess.

**Design that would make the test trustworthy** (adds to IDEAS E1 and the first-pass memo's verifier note):

1. Sealed evaluator: harness, node counter, propagation and verifier outside the evolvable file; score on work (propagation calls and node expansions, or CPU time on a pinned core), never on nodes alone, since a hook can buy nodes with hidden propagation.
2. Fresh seeds every generation, drawn from the planted generator at the critical colour count of each n (growth-law: 4.65, 5.99, 7.32, 8.63, 9.94 for n = 6..10), so no instance is seen twice; three disjoint seed pools: evolution, selection, and a final test touched once.
3. Fitness is the slope, not the level: fit log work against n over at least four training sizes and score the slope with its standard error, so a program cannot win on 6x6 constants. Growth over 6..8 is mostly surface (growth-law memo: at n = 8 surface terms are as large as the bulk), so a slope fitted there must be checked on held-out sizes.
4. Held-out sizes: 9x9 and 10x10 at their critical counts, never seen in evolution, with caps high enough that neither arm is censored (Track 1 and A22 were censored at 1e7 to 1e8 at 9x9 and 10x10; plan per-size caps of 1e9 or a resumable study, Track 9).
5. Controls at equal evaluations: random mutations without selection (Zhang et al. 2024), `bb-mrv`, and plain row-major DFS in wall time (it solved 9x9 c=11 in 2 to 6 s where `bb-mrv` took 6,772 s, ROUTES section 1).
6. Per-depth check: compare the selected program's per-depth node profile with the first-moment Z_d of row scan on the same instances; a gain concentrated before the peak (in the frame) is the only kind that can matter at 16x16.
7. Kill (preregistered): the selected program's work ratio against `bb-mrv` on held-out 10x10 is not below its ratio on 8x8 with a CI excluding equality; or its held-out gain is under half its training gain; or the random-mutation control is within its CI.

**Cheapest experiment that could kill it.** Before any evolution, the static half: score a few hundred static orders on A(S) with the real colour counts at 16x16 (minutes on one core). If none beats row scan by a digit, the static half is closed. The dynamic half needs the design above, about 15 to 20 core-hours plus model calls (IDEAS E1 budget).

**Odds.** That it beats `bb-mrv` on held-out 10x10 work at all, with a ratio that improves with n: about 10%. That it delivers 56 bits at 16x16: under 0.5%.

### 4.2 A learned ranker of partial frames (the inference view, tested directly)

**Statement.** A network trained on painted 16x16 boards with the official colour counts ranks the true frame among frames that pass the four corner blocks with R bits of ranking information, where R is well above the 2 bits measured for classical ranking (ROUTES section 3, "Cheap information about the frame": about 2 ranking bits, z = 1.40).

**Cost in bits.** Frames: log2(4.05e37) = 124.9 bits (A5 exact count); passing the corner blocks removes 17.45 bits, leaving 107.5 bits. Interior given the true frame: 10^8.3 nodes = 27.6 bits, and a wrong frame was independently re-checked to cost about as much to refute (ROUTES section 3, frame-first row; disputed by the 2026-11-13 memo). Frame-ranked search ≈ 107.5 - R + 27.6 = 135.1 - R bits, against 134.4 for row scan. R is the guess. Reaching the 1,000 core-year line needs R ≈ 56 to 66.

**Why it might fail immediately.** Section 3.3: under quiet planting the learned ranker reproduces colour rarity and corner information. Training data is free for positives (true frames from the painter) and almost free for negatives (with five clues a random blocks-passing frame has a completion with probability on the order of E[Z] for a random set, so label noise is negligible), so the Yehuda et al. barrier does not apply. This is the cleanest test of whether learning extracts anything the first moment misses.

**Cheapest kill.** Generate 2,000 painted 16x16 boards at the official colour counts (training) and 200 more (test). For each test board, sample 2^20 uniform blocks-passing frames as decoys (uniform frame sampling by the transfer-matrix count used in A5) and record the true frame's rank percentile. R estimated as -log2(median percentile), resolvable up to about 20 bits. Kill if R < 6 bits, or if R does not beat a logistic model on colour-rarity and corner-block features by at least 3 bits. Cost: a few GPU-hours or tens of CPU-hours, no search. Overlaps the "Border candidates ordered by first-ring fill count" item in ROUTES "Looks untried", which is the hand-written version.

**Odds.** R ≥ 6: about 5%. R ≥ 56: under 0.1%.

### 4.3 Nothing else transfers

- AlphaZero-style single-player search: the published regime is 50-item bin packing, 5x5 image jigsaws and the Rubik's cube where it is slower than the classical solver; its value network would need to be a frame ranker, which is candidate 2.
- Diffusion and recurrent Sudoku solvers: 9x9 instances that need at most one guess. Nothing in the papers addresses a propagation-blind instance or a 1,024-value domain.
- FunSearch and AlphaEvolve on the matched-edge score: that is the 470 regime (ROUTES section 7, local search and learned priors rows); the graded score exists but, per path 4, points away from solutions.
- NeuroCore-style learned core prediction for refuting wrong frames: the D1 track measures cores on the real pieces now; NeuroCore's own ablation says the benefit was the refocusing, not the network. If D1 finds small local cores, a classical core heuristic is the first thing to test, not a network.
