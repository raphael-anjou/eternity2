# Solve path 3: can exact search change its exponent?

Night 2026-11-12. Lens: time-memory trade-offs, frontier dynamic programming,
separators, interchangeability, tree-size estimation. Scripts and raw outputs:
`research/program/night/2026-11-12/memos/solve-path-3/`.

## Verdict in six lines

1. No region-based decomposition changes the exponent. On a frame-aware
   first-moment model of the official set, plain scan-order depth-first search
   costs 10^40.5 nodes with the five clues; the best two-list seam join costs
   10^39.7 and needs about 10^38 stored entries. The gain is 0.8 digits.
2. The reason is one sentence: a join can add two rising flanks instead of
   walking them in sequence, but every list it needs is itself a region count,
   and every region between one third and two thirds of the board has at
   least about 10^31 valid fills.
3. The site's conclusion on meet in the middle holds. Its numbers do not: its
   blob model (10^66 one-sided, 10^61.5 half board) is about 20 orders above
   the frame-anchored counts (10^40.5 and 10^39.3 with five clues).
4. Budget gap: 23 orders for three desktops for a year, 21.7 orders for 1,000
   core-years (at 10^8 nodes per core-second, an optimistic rate).
5. What the same model does show: the cost is almost all "which piece goes in
   which zone". Given the correct frame, the interior costs 10^14.8 nodes
   (about 70 core-days). Given the top/bottom split of the pieces, each half
   peaks at 10^9.9 instead of 10^39.8.
6. So the exponent can only move through information about piece zones. In
   clue units: the puzzle is 8 early clues (about 73 bits) short of 1,000
   core-years. I found no sound source for those bits inside exact search.

## 1. The approach examined, stated so it can be built

I looked for the best exact structure of this family and priced it. It is the
two-list seam join with an exact set key ("sliced seam join").

**Decomposition.** A horizontal seam after row r-1. Tops are valid fills of
rows 0..r-1, bottoms are valid fills of rows r..15, each with the clue cells
pinned.

**Why two lists and not four.** The piece-used-once rule is an equality key
only for a two-way split: the bottom's piece set must be the exact complement
of the top's, so `(piece set, seam colours)` is a hash key and the join costs
one probe per entry. With three or more parts the rule becomes "pairwise
disjoint and jointly exhaustive", which no key expresses before two parts
have been merged, and the merged list is a half-board list again.

**Algorithm.**
1. Give every piece a random 64-bit weight w_i; let W be the sum of all.
   Key of a top: `h = (sum of w over its pieces, seam vector)`; key of a
   bottom: `(W - sum, seam vector)`. Equal sums with equal seams imply
   complementary sets up to a 2^-64 collision, checked on the 256-bit masks.
2. Enumerate tops by row-major DFS from the top-left, bottoms by row-major
   DFS from the bottom-left (both keep the frame anchored, which matters, see
   section 2).
3. Slice: process only keys with `sum mod M = t`, for t = 0..M-1, so that one
   slice of tops fits in memory; each slice is a sort-merge or a hash join.
4. Report every joined pair; it is a full solution.

**Data structures.** Entry = 32-byte piece mask + 16 seam colours packed in
10 bytes + 8-byte sum, 50 bytes, or 16 bytes if only the 128-bit hash of
(sum, seam) is stored and the mask is recomputed on a hit. DFS engine: the
existing strict solver in `engine/src`, with a leaf callback at depth 16r.

**The catch that the cost model exposes.** The slice key is not pushable into
the enumeration: a residue of the piece-set sum is known only at the leaf. So
slicing divides memory by M and multiplies regeneration by M, unless the
lists are written to disk once. And each list is generated by a DFS that must
climb its own rising flank. Both points are priced below.

## 2. Cost model for the real 16x16

### The model

`solve-path-3/model.py`, `bulge16.py`, output in `bulge16.out`.

N(region) = (ordered piece choices) x 4^(free interior cells) x P_int(e_i) x
P_frame(e_f), where e_i and e_f are the numbers of matched interior-colour and
frame-colour edges inside the region, and the P are exact match probabilities
in the fixed-multiset ensemble (colours of the half-edges are a random
arrangement of the official multiset). This is the ensemble of the canonical
solution count (`web/content/research/build/analysis/solution-counting.mdx`,
lines 284 to 296; a model value, reported from the community, formula on the
page). Piece choices use three pools read from
`engine/data/official_eternity2.csv` (committed): 4 corners, 56 edge pieces
(one orientation each), 196 interior pieces (four orientations), minus pinned
clues.

Assumptions, all named:
- A1. First moment only: N is an expected count over the ensemble, not a count
  on the official instance.
- A2. A DFS with forward checking visits about N(d) nodes at depth d. Stronger
  propagation is not modelled.
- A3. Clue cells have one choice; the clue pieces' own colours are not
  conditioned on.
- A4. Node rate 10^8 per core-second (the site reports 52 to 110 million per
  thread, `web/content/research/build/faster/solver-engineering.mdx` lines 493
  and 513, uncommitted measurements). Three desktops = 48 cores for a year =
  10^17.2 nodes. 1,000 core-years = 10^18.5 nodes.

Two calibrations against committed or canonical numbers:
- Full board, centre clue only: log10 S = 4.167, that is 14,702. Five clues:
  10^-7.39 = 4.1e-8 (canonical: 4e-8). Reproduced.
- Number of valid frames: model 10^37.71; committed exact count 10^37.608
  (`research/experiments/frame-count-corners/README.md`, line 133, committed
  data). The model is 0.10 digits high.

### Two-list seam join

A list of tops for seam r has N_top(r) entries but costs the sum of the scan
profile up to depth 16r to generate, because the DFS that produces it climbs
its own flank. Same for bottoms. From `bulge16.out`, five clues:

| seam r | tops (log10) | bottoms (log10) | generation cost | DFS | gain in digits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 33.9 | 38.5 | 40.33 | 40.52 | 0.18 |
| 7 | 37.0 | 38.2 | 39.69 | 40.52 | 0.83 |
| 8 | 39.3 | 36.7 | 40.44 | 40.52 | 0.08 |
| 9 | 38.2 | 37.0 | 41.33 | 40.52 | -0.81 |

Best case: r = 7, 10^39.7 time, 10^37.0 stored entries of 16 bytes = 10^38.2
bytes. Centre clue only: best gain 1.05 digits at r = 7. A join that cannot
stop early (it has no first-solution shortcut on the many-solution variant
except by subsampling both lists by sqrt(S), a 2.1-digit saving against the
4.2 digits DFS gets from stopping at the first of 14,703) is strictly worse
there.

There is a tempting misreading of the table. With five clues the bottom 14
rows have only 10^18.3 valid fills and the top two rows 10^20.4: two short
lists whose join is the solution. But the short bottom list cannot be
produced cheaply. Enumerating the valid fills of 14 rows from a pool with 32
spare pieces is the original problem again: the DFS that lists them passes
through 10^39.2. The falling flank is small because the pool is nearly
exhausted, and reaching that state is the hard part. Small lists exist at
both ends; nothing connects them.

### Four quadrants and general join trees

Each 8x8 corner quadrant has 10^28.8 fills with its clue (10^26.1 for the one
that also holds the centre clue). Joining two neighbours gives the half-board
list again (28.8 + 28.8 - 9.3 seam - 9.4 disjointness = 39.0, against 39.3
computed directly). The disjointness filter is the issue: two independent
quadrant sets are disjoint with probability 10^-9.4, four quadrant sets
partition the pieces with probability 10^-93, and none of it is hashable
before a pair has been materialised. The site's 4-cycle argument
(`meet-in-the-middle.mdx`, lines 234 to 253, uncommitted model) reaches the
same conclusion; its figure of 10^80 intermediate rows is not reproducible
from the page, mine is 10^39 per materialised half plus 10^-9.4 wasted probes
per kept pair.

General statement (holds for any binary join tree over cells, under A1): some
node of the tree covers between 86 and 171 cells, and the list at that node
is the set of valid fills of that region. For band regions the smallest such
count is 10^31.5 (five clues, 86 cells). I did not minimise over all region
shapes; quadrant-like shapes are worse than bands at equal area (28.8 for 64
cells in a corner square against 25.5 for a 64-cell band). So no join tree
does better than about 10^31, and the only one with a hashable distinctness
rule, the two-list tree, sits at 10^39.7.

### Frontier dynamic programming, state merging, signatures

State = (seam colours, set of used pieces). Merging helps only if several
fills share a state. Under the model the number of available states at the
half board is 10^18.6 seams x 10^72.9 sets against 10^39.3 fills, so
independent coincidences have probability about 10^-52; merging can only
come from local rearrangements (two blocks of the fill with identical outer
colours, swapped). On the official set no two pieces are equal, so the
smallest such block is a pair; I estimate a merge factor below 2 (argued, not
measured). A transposition table buys under one digit.

Compressed signatures (colour-count vectors in place of sets) are not exact:
they are a hash partition of the same lists. They divide memory, not time.
The count vector of a top is also free information: local validity already
fixes every colour count's parity (count = 2 x internal edges + seam edges),
which is why colour-balance pruners never fire in strict search.

Relaxing distinctness to get a small DP state is priced by one formula. A
relaxation that lets r remaining pieces be drawn independently (transfer
matrix, tensor network, mean-field weights) inflates the completion count by
r^r / r!, about 10^(0.43 r): 10^12.7 at r = 32, 10^29.9 at r = 72, 10^63.7
at r = 150. A wrong prefix at the end of row 4 (centre clue) has an expected
10^(4.2 - 31.5) = 10^-27 exact completions and 192 pieces still to place; a
relaxation that inflates by 10^82 reports about 10^55 completions for it and
prunes nothing. Such a test can only refute where r is below about 30, where
DFS is already free.

### Where the exponent is: the conditional ledger

All from `bulge16.out`, five clues unless stated.

| condition | cost (log10 nodes) | information supplied (digits) |
|---|---|---|
| nothing | 40.5 | 0 |
| correct frame given | 14.8 (centre clue only: 21.1) | 37.6 |
| top/bottom split of the pieces given | 9.9 per half (peak, set by the top frame row) | 72.9 |
| one more true clue in rows 1 to 6 | minus 2.88 each | 2.88 (9.6 bits) |

Reading: 25.7 of the 40.5 digits are the price of not knowing the frame; the
interior alone is a 70 core-day problem. Per-cell law: cutting the eligible
pool of every cell by a factor rho cuts the count at depth k by rho^k. To
remove 22 digits over the 135 cells before the peak needs rho = 0.69 at every
cell without ever excluding the true piece: 73 bits of true side information.
Guessing does not supply it: a random restriction keeps the solution with
probability rho^256 and shrinks the tree by rho^k with k below 256, a net
loss. Branching on zone membership directly is worse than DFS: there are
10^72.9 top/bottom splits against 10^39.3 top fills, so DFS is already the
efficient enumerator of feasible splits. The 2011 community result on
checkerboard-balanced sets says the same from the other side: about 3 x
10^147 balanced partitions against 10^4 solutions
(`web/content/research/build/analysis/parity-arguments.mdx`, lines 189 to
200, reported).

**Single assumption that would remove most of the orders:** a method that
ranks the true frame among the first 10^3.7 candidates of the 10^37.6 valid
frames, or equivalently supplies 8 true placements in rows 1 to 6. With that,
1,000 core-years suffice on this model. I put under 1% on such a method
existing without design knowledge of the puzzle, because under the ensemble
the frame is recognised only by the interior, and refuting a wrong frame
costs as much as confirming the right one (10^14.8 expected nodes each).

## 3. Why it is not already known to fail, and what the literature says

**Closest prior attempt on the site.** BANDSAW
(`web/content/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/meet-in-the-middle/bandsaw.mdx`)
and the concept page (`build/exact/meet-in-the-middle.mdx`). EVIDENCE_AUDIT
grades the stop as argued. What they had: a band join on a 10x10 testbed with
a mismatch budget; a blob cost formula T(a,k) with minimum-perimeter regions.
What differs here: (a) frame-anchored regions with three piece pools and the
exact-multiset ensemble, which lowers every count by about 20 orders and
contradicts the page's claim that corner anchoring "prices strictly worse"
(lines 224 to 232): by a hand count, anchoring 7 rows to the frame costs 25
digits of frame choices and returns 35 digits of matched edges against a
free interior band; (b) generation cost of each list counted, which is
what limits the gain to 0.8 digits; (c) a check by exact enumeration,
committed as scripts. Result: the page's verdict stands, its numbers and one
of its arguments do not.

**Literature, each opened tonight.**
- Edmonds, Edmonds, Mertz, "Search Time when Solving Random Jigsaw Puzzles
  with a Planted Solution", undated manuscript, York University and
  University of Toronto,
  https://www.eecs.yorku.ca/~jeff/research/tsot/puzzle/puzzle.pdf (read in
  full, 15 pages; no DOI or venue found). Model: n pieces, q = sqrt(4n/eps)
  edge types, no separate frame palette, pool depletion approximated away
  ((4n)^t). Their eps = 4n/q^2 is the expected number of wrong pieces fitting
  a two-neighbour slot. Theorem 5: a rectangle grown from a corner has
  expected width 1 + Theta(eps). Theorems 2 to 4: frame first, a block in the
  middle and a corner triangle blow up. Theorem 6: with more compatible
  pairs, every order is exponential. **Where the official puzzle sits:** its
  interior has eps = 4 x 196 / 17^2 = 2.71 at the start, above 1, so it is in
  the Theorem 6 regime, not the Theorem 5 one; a unique-regime interior of
  196 pieces would need at least 28 colours. Their growth factor eps per cell
  is the rising flank of the bulge; the official puzzle leaves it only
  through depletion, when 4 x (pool left) / 289 drops below 1, at 72 interior
  pieces left. With the true frame pinned the same quantity explains the
  ledger: the count still grows, but from 1 instead of from 10^25. **On seam
  methods the paper says nothing**: it analyses one-sided recursive
  backtracking only (no tables, no join). Its Theorem 6 proof does use a
  checkerboard split of pieces, as a proof device. Its indirect message for
  seams: in the unique regime both lists would have O(1) width and a join
  would be pointless; in the many-solutions regime both lists grow like
  eps^t, which is the flank a join cannot avoid.
- Martinsson, "A linear threshold for uniqueness of solutions to random
  jigsaw puzzles", Combinatorics, Probability and Computing 28 (2019) 287 to
  302, arXiv:1701.04813 (abstract page opened). Uniform model, n x n, q edge
  types, no frame: at least two non-similar solutions for q <= (2/sqrt e) n,
  all solutions similar for q >= (2 + eps) n. For a 14-side interior
  (2/sqrt e) x 14 = 16.98 against 17 interior colours: the official interior
  sits on the lower threshold, which is the same fact as an expected count
  near 10^4. The paper is about counting, not search, and has no frame class,
  so nothing transfers to seams.
- Horowitz and Sahni 1974 (doi:10.1145/321812.321823, cited on the site, not
  reopened); Howgrave-Graham and Joux, "New generic algorithms for hard
  knapsacks", EUROCRYPT 2010, ePrint 2010/189 (page opened): 2^(0.311 n) by
  writing one solution as a sum of two partial ones in many ways. I worked
  the analogue: a family of R cuts gives R representations, but lists for
  different regions share no entries, so the filtered list size is the
  average region count, never below the best single cut. No gain.
- Dinur, Dunkelman, Keller, Shamir, "Efficient Dissection of Bicomposite
  Problems with Cryptanalytic Applications", CRYPTO 2012, ePrint 2012/217
  (page opened). Dissection improves the time-memory product when
  sub-problems are independent given guessed interface states. Here guessed
  interfaces do not make the parts independent (shared pool), and the time,
  not the product, is the binding term.
- Bosboom, Demaine, Demaine, Hesterberg, Manurangsi, Yodpinyanee, "Even 1 x n
  Edge-Matching and Jigsaw Puzzles are Really Hard", arXiv:1701.00146
  (abstract opened): NP-hard already in one row, so a narrow frontier alone
  does not make the piece-set coupling tractable.
- Benoist and Bourreau, "Fast Global Filtering for Eternity II", Constraint
  Programming Letters 3 (2008) 36 to 49, HAL lirmm-00364330 (record found by
  search, text not opened): constant-time filtering, a constant factor.
- Two web searches for meet in the middle, bidirectional or frontier dynamic
  programming on edge-matching puzzles returned no published attempt.

## 4. What is new

Nothing that solves the puzzle. New relative to the site, as far as I could
check:
1. A frame-aware first-moment bulge with exact multiset probabilities, which
   reproduces both 14,702 and the committed frame count, as a 150-line script.
2. The priced statement that a seam join gains at most 0.8 to 1.05 digits,
   and why (generation cost; flat top; no first-solution shortcut).
3. The join-tree bound (a one-third to two-thirds node always exists).
4. The conditional ledger: frame given costs 10^14.8; split given costs
   10^9.9; one early clue is 2.88 digits; the puzzle is 8 early clues from
   1,000 core-years.
5. The inflation law 10^(0.43 r) for any relaxation that forgets
   distinctness on r pieces, which bounds where tensor or transfer methods
   can prune.

## 6. First experiment

Under 10 core-hours, boards of at most 8x8, compiled engine (the strict
solver in `engine/src` with a leaf callback).

**Design.** Planted framed boards, no clue: 6x6 (4/5), 10 seeds; 7x7 with a
palette chosen by the model for about 10^10 nodes, 10 seeds; 8x8 (5/8), 3
seeds (model 10^11.5 nodes each, times the planted excess). For each board:
(a) exhaustive per-depth counts in one scan direction (four directions at
6x6 and 7x7); (b) nodes to generate the top list and the bottom list for
every seam r, counted, not stored; (c) the frame-given interior search and
the planted-pool half search. Total about 2.6 x 10^12 nodes, 7 core-hours at
10^8 nodes per second. Three seeds at 8x8 make a weak median; the 7x7 rung
carries the statistics.

**Kill line for the seam join, written before the run.** Let G(n) be the
median over seeds of (DFS nodes of the best scan) / (list-generation nodes at
the best seam). If G(8) < 10 and G(8)/G(6) < 3, the seam join is a constant
factor that does not grow with size: stop all work on seam, quadrant and
dissection joins for strict search. The model predicts G between 2 and 10
with no growth, so I expect the kill.

**Kill line for the ledger.** If the frame-given interior search costs more
than 100 times the model on more than 2 of 10 seeds at 8x8, or the model's
free-search total is off by more than a factor 10 from the exhaustive count
at 7x7, the model is not a usable instrument and the 16x16 figures in section
2 should not be quoted.

**What a surprise would look like.** G growing by 3 or more per size step
would mean the generation-cost argument is wrong and the join deserves a
10x10 run.

## 7. Probabilities

- Three desktops, one year, this approach: **10^-6**. Inside the model the
  number is 10^-23 (budget over tree size); the 17 orders in between are my
  allowance for the model being wrong about this designed instance in a
  favourable direction, which the small boards do not support (they err the
  other way).
- 1,000 core-years: **10^-5**. Inside the model, 10^-21.7.

Main reason they are not higher: no structure in this family changes the
rate. Every list a join needs is a region count on the rising flank, and the
only quantity that moves the exponent, zone information about pieces, has no
sound source in exact search.

## 8. Best idea rejected

Remove the piece-set from the state algebraically: count tilings by
inclusion-exclusion over which pieces are allowed (Ryser style), with a
colour-only transfer matrix inside, so the state is the 16-colour seam and
nothing else. It is a true change of exponent: the cost is 2^(number of
labels) x 10^18.6 seam states, independent of the bulge. With 256 labels that
is 10^77 x 10^18.6; even a square-root trick on the labels leaves 10^38.5 x
10^18.6 = 10^57, above plain DFS at 10^40.5. As a completion test under a DFS
prefix it only pays where 2^r is small, the last 30 pieces, where DFS is free.

## Open points and things I did not check

- The model is a first moment. The variance of the tree size on the official
  instance, and the planted excess at 16x16, are unmeasured.
- The join-tree bound was evaluated on bands and corner squares, not
  minimised over region shapes.
- Scan orders other than the four row and column scans were not priced; a
  better order shifts the DFS baseline, not the comparison.
- The merge factor on the official set (section 2) is an estimate.
- The node rate of 10^8 per core-second is the top of the site's reported
  range and is uncommitted.
