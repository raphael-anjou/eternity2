## 1. The analog

The field has four problems with this structure. None is exact; each fails at a named point.

**A. Regular syndrome decoding (closest formal match). [DERIVED]** Eternity II is a linear system with a weight constraint. Take one 0/1 unknown x(c, p, o) per cell c, piece p and orientation o allowed in that cell: 196 x 784 + 56 x 56 + 4 x 4 = 156,816 unknowns (clues ignored). Work over F_257. Three families of linear checks:
- one row per cell: the unknowns of that cell sum to 1 (256 rows);
- one row per piece: the unknowns of that piece sum to 1 (256 rows; a count in 0..256 that is 1 mod 257 is exactly 1, so the each-tile-once rule is linear);
- one row per internal edge: (colour shown by the cell on one side) minus (colour shown by the cell on the other side) = 0 (480 rows; colours are 1..22, so equality mod 257 is equality).

A solution is a 0/1 vector of weight 256 with exactly one nonzero per block of unknowns (one block per cell) satisfying 992 checks (rank at most 991, not computed). That is the regular syndrome decoding problem: "a solution to the Syndrome Decoding (SD) problem follows a particular block-wise structure" (source 11, abstract). Mapping: tiles and orientations are column labels; the all-different constraint is 256 ordinary parity checks, not a separate object; the planted solution is the planted error vector; the density "about one solution with five clues" is the uniqueness bound of the code (source 11, Proposition 3.1 names it "uniqueness bound for RSD").
Where it breaks: the code has rate 1 - 992/156,816 = 0.9937 and relative weight 0.0016, a corner of parameter space where generic decoders are at their worst (section 3, barrier B1), and the check matrix is sparse and local (each edge row touches two blocks) where the decoding literature assumes a random matrix. Backtracking is the algorithm that uses that locality; generic decoding ignores it.

**B. Multiple encryption, the composite problem of Dinur, Dunkelman, Keller and Shamir. [DERIVED mapping]** Rows of the board are the r = 16 rounds; the 16 pieces placed in a row are the round key; the 16 colours on the row's lower side are the intermediate state (14 x log2 17 + 2 x log2 5 = 61.9 bits, the 10^18.6 of path 3). Where it breaks: section 2b, D2. In short, the round keys are not independent (shared pool) and a round is a sparse relation, not a permutation of the state.

**C. The Permuted Kernel Problem. [CITED, abstract only]** Find a permutation of a known vector that lies in the kernel of a known matrix; "an NP-hard algebraic problem which has been extensively studied" (source 16). It is the field's standard problem whose secret is a permutation, that is, an all-different constraint over all positions. Eternity II is a permuted-kernel instance whose matrix is the sparse local one of analog A, with orientations added. Where it breaks: PKP matrices are dense and random and the attacks named in source 16 (Joux and Jaulmes, Patarin and Chauvaud, Poupard) are tuned to that; I did not open them.

**D. Lattice enumeration for a planted short vector. [CITED for the lattice side, DERIVED for the mapping]** A depth-first tree over coordinates with a pruning rule, searched for one planted leaf, with a first-moment estimate (the Gaussian heuristic) that predicts the node count per level. That is the row-scan tree and its model. Where it breaks: section 2b, D5. The lattice tree counts every partial vector inside a ball whose radius is a budget shared by all levels; strict edge matching has no shared budget, so the mechanism of extreme pruning has no object in strict search and has one only in break-tolerant search.

**D1. Trade-offs and memory-limited joins.** Hellman's scheme pays N once and inverts many targets cheaply (source 1). Here N is the search itself and there is one target, so nothing is amortised. The within-instance form (reuse of refutations) is the no-good row of ROUTES, flagged undecided there.
Memory-limited join by golden collision (source 4, equation 8): with both lists at the reviewer's floor n1 = n2 = 10^38.1 = 2^126.6 and one processor, T = 7 x 2^126.6 x 2^63.3 / sqrt(w) = 2^(192.7 - 0.5 lg w). At w = 2^50 words: 2^167.7. At w = 2^60: 2^162.7. T reaches 2^134.4 only at w = 2^116.5. And the method assumes "bijective mappings" from integers to list entries; a valid half board cannot be produced from its index without a search. Verdict: not applicable, and 33 bits worse than depth-first search if it were.

**D2. Dissection: what applies, what bicomposite structure is required, and the bound.**
[CITED] Source 5 requires "two orthogonal ways in which we can decompose a given problem into (almost) independent parts": "the keys are independently chosen and the plaintexts are independently processed", so that "if we know certain subsets of keys and certain subsets of intermediate values, we can independently verify their consistency". For the knapsack and for a Rubik's-cube-like permutation puzzle they build an execution matrix whose rows are freely chosen steps and whose columns evolve independently given the step (section 1). Their gain is in memory at given time: the baseline curve is TM = 2^(rn) "for any values T, M such that M <= 2^(floor(r/2) n)", and dissection gives for example T = 2^(4n), M = 2^n at r = 7. In their Table 1 the time exponent for r = 38 falls from 30 at M = 2^n to 19 = r/2 at M = 2^(10n); I found no entry of the table below r/2, that is, below the time of the full-memory meet in the middle.
[DERIVED] On Eternity II with rows as rounds:
1. Rows are not independent steps. The legal fills of row i depend on which pieces rows 1..i-1 used, not only on the seam colours. Independence returns only if the state also carries the piece set, which makes the state 61.9 + 247 bits wide (247.2 = lg of C(4,2) x C(56,28) x C(196,98), the top/bottom split count; path 3 has 10^72.9 with clues).
2. A guessed state is almost never reachable. In a cipher every key maps every state to a state, so a guessed middle state always has 2^(kn - n) preimages on each side and guessing it wastes nothing. Here path 3's numbers give 10^18.6 seams x 10^72.9 sets = 10^91.5 states against 10^39.3 reachable ones: a fraction 10^-52.2. The literal dissection step (guess the middle state, solve both sides independently at 10^9.9 each) costs 10^(91.5 + 9.9) = 10^101.4 = 2^337.
3. The column direction partly holds: seam colours can be guessed column by column. The piece set cannot be checked by column.
Does dissection change the 1 to 2.4 digit bound? No. The bound is a time bound: a two-list band join pays at least the larger list, 10^38.1. Dissection never lowers the time of a full-memory join; it lowers memory at equal or larger time. And depth-first search already sits at zero memory within 2.4 digits of that floor, so the whole time-memory curve of this instance fits in a 2.4-digit window. There is nothing for dissection to trade. This also disposes of "dissection into seam-guessed bands" (IDEAS rank 11) for strict search.

**D3. Generalised birthday.** One correction to path 3 first. Its section 1 says the each-tile-once rule is a hash key only for a two-way split. That is not so: write each part's used set as a 256-bit indicator vector; since the part sizes add up to 256, k parts partition the pieces exactly when their indicators XOR to the all-ones vector. So the rule is a k-XOR condition and Wagner's tree, Schroeppel and Shamir, and dissection joins are all well defined for k = 4 or more. The reviewer had flagged that argument as unproved (`price-and-ledger.REVIEW.md`, section E). The verdict survives for a different reason:
- The matching condition is as wide as the partition entropy H_k. From the three pools: H_2 = 247.2 bits, H_4 = 488.5 bits (four bands of four rows), H_8 = about 716 bits (eight bands of two rows). Seam conditions come on top and are ignored here, which favours the method.
- [CITED] The k-tree needs lists of size 2^(n/(1+lg k)) for an n-bit condition and works "only when ... there are sufficiently many solutions" (source 6).
- [DERIVED] List size 2^(H_k/(1+lg k)) = 2^123.6, 2^162.8, 2^179.0 for k = 2, 4, 8. It grows with k because H_k grows like 256 lg k while the divisor grows like 1 + lg k. The number of solutions the tree needs is 2^(k x list bits - H_k): 1 for k = 2, 2^163 for k = 4. The instance has about 1 (five clues), 2^13.8 (one clue), 2^23.5 (none).
- With one solution, a k-list method must cover all pairs of the two larger halves, which is the two-list floor again.
- The record regime does have many "solutions" (10^34.4 = 2^114 boards at 470, ROUTES section 5). Even there the k = 4 requirement of 2^163 is not met, and the lists would have to be sampled at unit cost, which nobody can do for valid quarter boards with a break budget.
Reading: in a join, the each-tile-once rule is a 247-bit matching condition between independently produced halves. A sequential search satisfies it by construction, because it draws from the remaining pool. That is why every list method here pays more than the tree.

**D4. Representations.** [CITED] The technique looks "for one of the many possible representations of a given solution" (source 8) and then adds a constraint that kills all but about one representation, which shrinks the lists by the number of representations.
[DERIVED] In analog A the checks are linear, so the filter exists: for a partial assignment x1 on a region S, the syndrome of x1 holds S's piece set, zero on S's internal edges, and the boundary colours; a random linear hash of it can be fixed to a random target. Take a family of R balanced regions S. The solution has R representations (S, complement). To keep about one, fix l = lg R hash bits. The filtered list then holds (sum over S of N(S)) / R = the mean of N(S) over the family, which is at least the minimum of N(S). So representations give a list no smaller than the best single cut, and the best band cut is the reviewer's 10^38.1. Path 3 reached the same conclusion in one sentence; the derivation here adds the reason it is general: in a random knapsack every subset is a legal partial object and the hash is the only filter, so enlarging the family is free; here the filter that makes lists small is internal consistency of a region, and it is tied to the region.
The "1+1=0" extension of source 9 (partial objects that overlap and cancel) has no counterpart that keeps internal consistency: two fills that overlap must agree on the overlap, which reduces the count of representations to 1 per pair of regions.
Assumption flagged: that band regions minimise N(S) among balanced regions. Path 3 checked bands and corner squares only.

**D5. Enumeration with extreme pruning: what rerandomisation would be here, and its ceiling.**
[CITED] Source 12 prunes so hard that the target survives with probability about 0.1%, gains more than a factor 1,000 in tree size, and repeats on reshuffled bases. The reason it pays (their section 5): "although the success probability is exponentially small, the volume of the search region decreases by a stronger exponential factor". The success probability is computable because the target's coordinates behave like a uniform direction (Heuristic 2), so the target's partial norms at level k concentrate near k/n of the budget while the tree at level k contains everything up to the full budget.
[DERIVED] Three statements.
1. Blind pruning loses, as in lattices. Restrict every cell's pool to a random fraction rho. The solution survives with probability rho^256; the tree at depth d shrinks by rho^d with d = 135 to 161 at the peak. At rho = 0.9: 38.9 bits lost, 20.5 to 24.5 gained. Path 3 has this; it is the same ledger as a blind restriction in a lattice, which also loses. Extreme pruning is never blind.
2. What plays the role of the bounding function. A pruning set A at depth d keeps the true prefix with probability p and a fraction f of the nodes; the cost becomes T x f / p. The gain is p/f, the likelihood ratio of "prefix of the solution" against "node of the tree" on A. By the planted-ensemble symmetry already used in `M12/solve-path-2-designed-instance.md` (Bayes' rule), the true prefix is a uniform draw from the completable prefixes. So p/f is exactly how well a cheap statistic predicts completability, the question of the frame-information memo. Strict matching has no budget shared across levels, so there is no free geometric statistic as in a lattice; one has to be found.
3. What rerandomisation is, and what it can buy. For a fixed instance and a fixed statistic, the solution's value is a fixed number; without rerandomisation, lowering the threshold until the solution passes yields exactly the "ranking bits" of the statistic (1.7 to 2.3 bits for the first-ring statistic, `M12/theory-and-frame.REVIEW.md` F5). Rerandomisation must redraw the statistic. Candidates: the four scan directions (row scan from each side: four nearly disjoint prefix regions, so four nearly independent draws); other prefix shapes (column bands, staircases), correlated with those; the choice of which clues are pinned first. Colour or piece relabelling does not count: it permutes siblings and leaves the tree and the statistic unchanged. If the statistic is Gaussian with a shift of delta standard deviations between completable and typical prefixes, and K independent presentations are run at the threshold t where one of them is expected to pass (Q(t - delta) = 1/K), the gain is (1/K) / Q(t):

| shift delta | K = 4 | K = 100 | K = 1,000 | K = 10^6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 sd | 1.1 bits | 2.1 | 2.6 | 3.7 |
| 1.4 sd (the frame statistic's z) | 3.7 | 6.7 | 8.1 | 11.4 |
| 3.0 sd | 11.0 | 17.6 | 20.8 | 27.7 |

(stdlib: `NormalDist().inv_cdf(1-1/K)+delta` for t, `0.5*erfc(t/sqrt(2))` for Q.) Roughly delta x sqrt(2 ln K) / ln 2 bits. Closing the 56 bits that ROUTES row 1 names as the reopening threshold needs delta = 5.2 at K = 2^20, which is a classifier good enough to solve the puzzle without extreme pruning. Extreme pruning amplifies a separation by about sqrt(2 ln K); it does not create one. The Gaussian tail is the guessed factor: a discrete statistic on 16 seam cells will have a shorter tail, which lowers these figures.
Two existing rows read differently under this lens. ProbCut (A12) was killed because "cuts removed planted branches": that is a pruned enumeration with low success probability and no rerandomisation, so the kill is what source 12 predicts for a single run. And the break schedule of the record engines (ROUTES section 7, "scheduled late breaks") is a bounding function in the exact sense of source 12: a non-decreasing cap on the budget spent by depth d, in a search that has a shared budget (the breaks). The record community arrived at pruned enumeration on its own; what it does not do is choose the schedule by minimising cost over success probability.

**D6. Information-set decoding on analog A.** Prange's step guesses a set of columns that contains the whole error and solves linearly. Tailored to blocks: keep c candidates per cell, with 256 c <= 992 so that the linear system pins the answer, c = 3.875. Success probability per guess: 3.875^256 / (784^196 x 56^56 x 4^4) = 2^(500.3 - 2217.7) = 2^-1717. Generic formula of source 10: cost 2^(c w) with c = -lg(1 - R) = -lg(992/156,816) = 7.30 and w = 256: 2^1870. Source 10 proves Stern, MMT and BJMM share that constant when w = o(n); here w/n = 0.0016. So the whole ISD family sits near 2^1700 to 2^1900 against 2^134.4. The 1,580 missing bits are the value of locality: the tree checks each edge the moment its two cells are filled; a generic decoder sees 992 unstructured checks. For "information-set decoding of an incumbent" (IDEAS rank 9): Prange needs an information set free of errors, and ROUTES section 5 (argued, not refereed) puts almost every 470 board 246 or more cells from any solution, so the error-free set does not exist for the solve.

**D7. Sieving and algebraic attacks.** Sieving needs a set closed under subtraction. The kernel lattice of analog A has dimension about 156,816 - 991; at 2^(0.292 n) (source 14) that is beyond any meaning. Even a hypothetical lattice of dimension 1,860 (the bit length of a board: lg(4! x 56! x 196! x 4^196) = 1,860.1) would cost 2^543. For algebraic solving, source 15 gives 2^(0.792 n) for a semi-regular boolean quadratic system with as many equations as unknowns. No quadratic model of the puzzle has only 1,860 unknowns, so 2^(0.792 x 1860) = 2^1473 is an optimistic floor for that class, not an estimate. The real one-hot model has 156,816 unknowns and about 10^8 monomial equations (pairs of mismatching neighbours), is far from semi-regular, and I found no degree-of-regularity estimate for systems of that shape; its Groebner computation under an elimination order that follows the row scan is the transfer matrix with the used set, which ROUTES section 4 prices at 10^43 to 10^50. Depth-first search runs at 2^(0.072 n) in the same unit (134.4 / 1860). Generic algebraic exponents are an order of magnitude above it.

**D8. One sentence on all of it.** Every method in this field that beats the square root of the key space does so on problems whose constraints are dense and whose partial objects are free; this instance already runs at 2^(0.072 x key bits) because its constraints are local and checked early, and the residual cost is a counting fact (the number of consistent partial fills at the widest cut), which no list method avoids because every list is such a count.

## 3. Hardness evidence

The field has no lower bound for a fixed instance and no unconditional lower bound for these problems at all. What it has are barriers for classes of algorithms. Each with its assumptions and whether they hold here.

- **B1. Sub-linear weight collapses all decoders onto Prange. [CITED, theorem]** Source 10: for w = o(n) "the constant c only depends of the code rate k/n and is the same for all the known ISD variants"; "our proof seems to indicate that any foreseeable variant of ISD should have the same asymptotic behavior". Source 11 says the same of the regular variant ("differs only by second order terms"). Assumptions: random linear code, asymptotic. Here: w/n = 0.0016, so the regime holds; the code is not random, so the theorem bounds generic decoders only. Consequence [DERIVED]: representations, the 2010 to 2012 advances of this field, are worth nothing on this instance through the decoding route (D6).
- **B2. The k-tree needs many solutions, and lower bounds for the single-solution case are missing. [CITED]** Source 6: works "only when one can extend the size of the lists freely"; Theorem 1 gives only Omega(2^(n/k)); "the existing lower bounds are very weak when k >> 2. Lacking provable lower bounds, we hope the importance of this problem will motivate researchers to search for credible conjectures". Assumptions: random lists, a group operation. Here: one solution, so the method is out by its own precondition (D3). This is a precondition, not a proof that nothing else works.
- **B3. Dissection is optimal in its own class and does not go under the join's time. [CITED, theorem within a framework]** Source 5, section 3.5: optimal "amongst all dissection algorithms that split the r rounds into two (not necessarily equal) parts and attacks each part recursively". Assumptions: independent keys, ideal ciphers. Here: assumptions fail (D2), and the conclusion that matters, no time below the two-list join, is the direction that carries over.
- **B4. Trade-offs cannot beat N^2 / M^2 online, and need N of precomputation. [CITED, theorem in a model]** Sources 2 and 3. Assumptions: random function, the stateful-random-graph model (source 2), preprocessing counted separately. Here: one instance, so the precomputation is the search.
- **B5. Extreme pruning has a floor. [CITED, theorem under the heuristic model]** Source 13: a lower bound on the success probability gives a lower bound on total running time. Assumptions: lattice geometry (cylinder intersections), Gaussian heuristic. Here: the theorem does not transfer; its message does, in the form derived in D5: the gain is bounded by the likelihood ratio the bounding function can reach.
- **B6. Parallel collision search with limited memory costs n^(3/2) / sqrt(w), not n. [CITED, heuristic plus simulation]** Source 4, equation 8. Here: 33 bits above the tree at any buildable memory (D1).
- **What is absent.** No result in this field says a planted instance at the uniqueness threshold on a grid is hard for all algorithms. The two closest statements are conjectural: the k-sum conjecture behind B2 (not opened today) and the working assumption of code-based cryptography that structured-but-not-too-structured codes decode no faster than random ones, which this instance violates in the favourable direction (its checks are local) and backtracking already collects that gain.

## 4. Transfer candidates

Two. I could not find a third that survives its own arithmetic.

**T1. Pruned row scan with deliberate low success rate, rerandomised over scan regions (extreme pruning, source 12).**
- Statement. There is a statistic s, computable in O(1) amortised per node on a row-scan prefix at rows 6 to 9, whose value on the planted prefix is shifted by delta >= 1.4 sd from its value on tree nodes at the same depth. If so, a row scan that keeps only nodes with s beyond a threshold, run from each of K scan presentations, costs 134.4 - G bits with G from the table in D5.
- Arithmetic. G = lg((1/K) / Q(t)), Q(t - delta) = 1/K. With the only shift measured so far (1.40, on whole frames, not on row-scan prefixes) and K = 4 directions: G = 3.7 bits, cost 2^130.7. With K = 100 region shapes, if they were independent: 6.7 bits. Guessed factors: that a shift of 1.4 exists at the peak (unmeasured), that presentations are independent (they overlap), and the Gaussian tail.
- Cheapest kill. Track 10's estimator already samples weighted nodes of the real tree, and the program has planted 16x16 boards painted from the official colour counts (20 in Track 10, three in D1). Preregister at most ten statistics (colour ledger of the remaining pool, number of remaining pieces that fit at least one seam slot, number of distinct seam colours, seam colour-pair supply, and so on). For each planted board and each of four scan directions, compute s on the planted prefix at depths 96, 112, 128 and on the sampled nodes at the same depth, weighted. 20 boards x 4 directions gives delta to about +-0.11. Kill if every |delta| is below 0.5 after correcting for ten statistics (then G <= 2.1 bits at K = 100). Under one core-hour once the sampler exposes its nodes; an exhaustive version at 8x8 costs seconds.
- Odds. A statistic with delta >= 1.4 at the peak: 5%. A gain of 10 bits or more: 1%. Closing the gap: below 0.1%. Evidence against, already in the repo: A12 (cuts remove planted branches at the same rate as others), A16 (AUC gain 0.010 over domain size), the path-4 ledger prune priced at 1 to 1.7 orders, arc consistency seeing nothing past the corners. The value of the test is that it measures delta directly, which converts several "killed, small boards" rows into one number on the real colour counts.

**T2. Choose the break schedule of the record engine by minimising cost over success, as a bounding function (record regime only; no bearing on a full solution).**
- Statement. The break schedule is a bounding function (D5). Source 12 chooses its function numerically to minimise (nodes per run) / (success probability per run) and accepts very low success per run. Doing the same with the partial-match model would give a schedule that is not Blackwood's hand-tuned list and a stated optimum for the quota share that REPORT recommendation 1 wants swept.
- Arithmetic. The partial-match referee puts the whole schedule at 22 decades, of which 20.6 belong to any single late gate and 1.5 to 2.3 to the shape of the list (`M12/partial-match-regime.REVIEW.md`, row D). So the ceiling of this candidate, inside the model, is the part of those 1.5 to 2.3 decades (5 to 7.6 bits) that the published list does not already collect. My guess for that part: 0 to 1 decade. That is the guessed factor.
- Cheapest kill. Minutes of stdlib: optimise the cap-by-depth vector in the memo's `model.py` under the cost-over-arrival objective and compare its predicted nodes per 470 with the published list's. Kill if the optimum is within a factor 3 of the published list in the model. If it passes, one evening on the port: legal nodes per arrival at depth 201, optimised schedule against default, 16 openings each (Track 15's design).
- Odds. Factor 3 or more in the model: 25%. Factor 3 or more on the port: 10%. A 471: not estimable from this; the model is about 6 decades off reported compute (ROUTES section 5).

**Nothing else transfers, and why.** Joins of every kind (meet in the middle, dissection, k-list, representations, collision search) pay the each-tile-once rule as a 247-bit matching condition that sequential search gets for free (D3), and their lists are region counts no smaller than the tree's widest cut (D2, D4). Decoding and algebraic methods price the instance as if its checks were dense and random and land above 2^1400 (D6, D7). Sieving has no closed set to work in (D7).
