A backtracker on Eternity II spends most of its effort not placing pieces but
ruling them out. Since [no cell is ever forced](/research/why/no-forced-moves)
(every interior position keeps dozens of live candidates deep into the
search), the only leverage available is shrinking those candidate lists as
hard and as cheaply as possible. Arc consistency is the standard machine for doing
that, and it has a fifty-year paper trail.

## Domains that police each other

Give every empty cell a *domain*: the set of piece-and-rotation candidates
still allowed there. On the full puzzle an interior cell starts with 784
tuples (196 interior pieces × 4 rotations, none of them rotation-symmetric);
an edge cell starts with at most 56 and a corner with at most 4, since the
grey side fixes the rotation. **Forward checking** is the one-step discipline: when a piece
is placed, delete it from every other domain and delete every candidate that
clashes with the newly exposed edges. Cheap, and every serious solver does at
least this.

**Arc consistency** demands more. For every pair of adjacent cells, every
candidate in one domain must have at least one compatible partner in the
other, a *support*. When a deletion anywhere leaves a candidate
unsupported, that candidate goes too, and the check ripples outward until
nothing more can be removed. For edge-matching this is exactly "a piece may
stay in a cell only if each neighbouring cell can still answer its colours".

## AC-3, and where it wastes work

Alan Mackworth's AC-3 (1977) is the coarse-grained way to reach that fixed
point: keep a queue of directed arcs, revise one arc at a time (scan a domain,
delete unsupported candidates), and whenever a domain shrinks, requeue the
arcs that point at it. It is short, correct, and easy to make incremental
inside a search, which is why it is still the default everywhere. Worst case
it costs $O(e d^3)$ for $e$ arcs and domain size $d$; on the 16×16 board
$e = 960$ (all 480 joins, both directions) and $d = 784$ for an interior
cell.

Its known flaw: every revision searches for supports from scratch, so the same
support gets rediscovered thousands of times as the search dives and
backtracks.

Two things are worth noticing. Early placements barely ripple: one ring of
neighbours shrinks and the wave stops, because the second ring still finds
supports for all three colours among the survivors. Late placements ripple
much further: domains are tight, and single deletions strand candidates two
and three cells away. That is exactly the behaviour on the real puzzle: arc
consistency earns its keep in the endgame, not the opening.

## Step by step

The same run, in words. This is all of AC-3:

1. **Start.** Every cell holds a domain of 64 candidates. The arc queue is
   empty; with no deletions yet, there is nothing to check.
2. **A piece lands on B2.** Its domain collapses to the single placed
   candidate. Every arc *aimed at* B2 enters the queue, one per neighbour:
   here (B1→B2), (C2→B2), (B3→B2), (A2→B2). Nothing else does: no other
   domain changed, so no other arc can have lost a support.
3. **Pop an arc, revise it.** Take (B1→B2): scan B1's 64 candidates, and for
   each one search B2's domain for a compatible partner, a *support*. B2
   now holds one piece, so only candidates whose south edge matches its north
   colour survive: roughly a third. The rest are deleted.
4. **Deletions re-arm arcs.** B1's domain shrank, so candidates elsewhere
   that leaned on the deleted ones may be stranded: every arc pointing at B1
   re-enters the queue, except the one from B2 that just fired. This is the
   ripple.
5. **No deletion, no requeue.** When a revision removes nothing, the arc is
   simply dropped. Early in the game the second ring almost always survives
   intact, and the queue drains in a handful of revisions.
6. **Fixed point.** The queue is empty: every candidate everywhere has a
   support in every neighbouring domain. No order of processing changes this
   final state: the fixed point is unique, and the queue discipline changes
   only how fast you reach it.

The AC-1 comparison in the demo is the entire argument for the worklist: the
naive loop re-revises all 48 arcs per sweep until one sweep is clean, doing
the same deletions for several times the checks, and the gap widens as the
board grows.

## What the stronger variants buy

The literature spent two decades fixing that redundancy. AC-4 (Mohr &
Henderson 1986) counts supports explicitly: optimal $O(e d^2)$ time, but
$O(e d^2)$ memory and painful state restoration on backtrack. AC-6 and AC-7
(Bessière; Bessière, Freuder & Régin) store supports lazily and exploit
bidirectionality, keeping optimal time with $O(e d)$ space at the price of
fine-grained bookkeeping.

The version worth knowing today is **AC-2001/AC-3.1**, found independently by
Bessière & Régin and by Zhang & Yap in 2001: keep AC-3's simple loop, but
remember for each candidate-arc pair the *last support found*, check that it
is still alive before searching again, and resume the scan from where it
stopped rather than from zero. That one integer per pair delivers the optimal
$O(e d^2)$ bound with only $O(e d)$ memory; on this puzzle that is
$960 \times 784 = 752{,}640$ small integers, negligible. The 2005 journal study
measures 1.5–9× less CPU than AC-3 in maintained-arc-consistency search on
generic binary CSPs.

Those generic bounds are context, not the right comparison here. Every
Eternity II constraint is colour equality between two facing half-edges, so a
candidate in cell $X$ is supported across the arc to $Y$ exactly when some
candidate of $Y$ shows the same colour on the facing side. Keep, for each
cell and side, a counter of live candidates per colour: a deletion decrements
one counter per side, and a counter reaching zero deletes the candidates of
the neighbour that needed that colour. No support is ever searched for, and
full arc consistency costs $O(e d)$ in total, an AC-4-style scheme
specialised to a colour channel of 22 values. That dominates AC-2001 on this
structure.

## The ceiling: why not path consistency?

Arc consistency checks pairs of cells. The next rung up, *path consistency*,
checks triples: it removes any pair of assignments that no third cell can
support, propagating a much stronger condition. On small boards it shrinks the search tree
enormously, and one community implementation measured how much it cost. In
March 2008 Geoff ran the ladder on Brendan Owen's beginner puzzles
([msg 4827](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/4827)):

- On the 6×6, plain nodal consistency leaves a search tree of over **40,000
  nodes**. Partial path-1 consistency drops it to about 10,000. Partial path-2
  consistency drops it to **138 nodes**, the bare minimum needed to walk to all
  eight solutions. But the runtime goes from 0.25 seconds to over **10 seconds**.
- On the 8×8 the trade gets worse. Arc consistency alone solves it in under
  **6 million nodes and about 36 seconds**. Adding path-1 preprocessing cuts the
  tree to roughly 1 million nodes but costs about **14 minutes** of
  preprocessing; path-2 preprocessing was still running after **7 hours**.

On those two boards, with that one 2008 implementation of *partial* path
consistency, each stronger level cut the tree by orders of magnitude and cost
more time than it saved. Geoff's own conclusion was that path consistency is
"simply not a cost-benefit positive effect" on this puzzle. That is
[prune versus speed](/research/why/prune-vs-speed) in the language of a real
algorithm. It is evidence from a 6×6 and an 8×8, not a measurement on the
16×16, and a better implementation could move the balance. The record
engines are a separate story: they run no arc consistency at all, because
their mismatch budget makes hard AC unsound (see below).

## What it costs

The generic accounting, with $e$ the number of directed arcs and $d$ the
maximum domain size:

- **AC-3** runs in $O(e d^3)$ time worst case: each arc can be re-enqueued up
  to $d$ times (once per deletion in its far domain), and each revision costs
  up to $d^2$ support checks. Its working memory is just the queue, $O(e)$.
- **AC-2001** runs in $O(e d^2)$ time, which is *optimal* for any algorithm
  based on arc revision: there are $e d$ candidate–arc pairs and each may
  need its support walked once across a domain of size $d$. The price is the
  last-support table: one integer per candidate–arc pair, $O(e d)$ space.

On Eternity II's scale those symbols are: $e = 960$ (all 480 joins, both
directions) and $d = 784$ candidate tuples per interior cell. So the generic
worst-case bounds sit near $e d^3 \approx 4.6 \times 10^{11}$ elementary
checks for AC-3 against $e d^2 \approx 5.9 \times 10^8$ for AC-2001. For this
puzzle's colour-equality constraints the counter scheme above needs only
$O(e d) \approx 7.5 \times 10^5$ counter updates for a full propagation from
scratch. Worst cases are pessimistic (real propagations touch a few cells, as
the demo above shows). The catch on this puzzle is not the propagation cost
per node; it is that inside a search this cost is paid at *every* node,
millions of times per second, which is why constant factors and cache
behaviour end up mattering as much as the exponent on $d$.

## Measured on this puzzle

- **The committed ablation.** The
  [DFS study](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/dfs-study/findings)
  ran an MRV backtracker with forward checking, with AC-3 over the frontier,
  and with AC-3 plus the per-colour supply check, on ten corner-pinned
  variants of the official puzzle, 60 seconds each on one core. Mean scores
  were 322.2, 320.6 and 320.6 matched edges of 480: indistinguishable at that
  budget, with a spread of about 11 points across instances. A plain
  row-major backtracker with no look-ahead averaged 376.6 in the same grid,
  because it visits millions of nodes per second where the MRV variants
  visit thousands. Those rates belong to the programs run (an untuned solver that copies its state at each branch), not to arc consistency itself. Reproduce with `just experiments dfs-study`.
- **One unreplicated run.** On an earlier version of this project's engine,
  AC-3 together with the
  [per-colour matching filter](/research/build/reduce/alldiff-regin)
  reached 449 of 480 edges on the canonical puzzle in one run of about 44
  seconds on a single thread. No forward-checking-only run under the same
  conditions exists, and the result is not committed, so it does not
  separate AC from the colour filter or from the rest of that engine.
- The unbuilt part: AC-2001 was recommended twice in this project's notes and
  never actually built, so its projected 2–5× win here is a reading of the
  literature, not a measurement. And the belief that AC-3 dominated the
  runtime profile was itself never confirmed by a profiler. Measure before
  porting.

## What the full-solve series measured under row scan

Priced against the row-scan tree of the real pieces with five clues (see
[approaches priced out](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/approaches-priced-out)), arc consistency is the
one propagation with a measured worth. Its gain is about 5.5 bits of nodes in
branches tried (6.1 on surviving nodes), estimated directly on the official
pieces; on generated toy boards of side 5 to 10 near the critical colour count
it is 4.5 to 4.8 bits, flat with size. That figure was independently
re-checked. Its cost per node is the other side: the desk study prices one
check at about 13 bits per node, a figure not independently checked, so the
net is a loss.

Where it refutes wrong boards is late. On generated 16×16 boards with the
official colour census (toy boards, never Eternity II progress), arc
consistency run alone on prefixes sampled from the row scan still accepted
0.427 of the wrong prefixes at free depth 96, 0.096 at 112 and 0.179 at 128,
that is it refuted 57, 90 and 82 percent; it refuted 94 percent at 144, 99 at
152 and all at 160, and it never refuted the true prefix. This follow-up was
**not independently checked**. A coarser run, independently reproduced, found
every sampled prefix refuted in 189 of 192 board-direction rows at depth 160
and in all 192 at depth 176. A share refuted is not a saving: a perfect free
cut at depth 144 would save at most 2.4 bits of nodes, as
[late filters](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/late-filters) works out.

## The soundness caveat

Arc consistency assumes every edge must match perfectly. The record engines
do not: [Blackwood-style searches](/research/lab/experiments/joshua-blackwood/solver) allow a
budget of deliberate mismatches at late depths, and under that regime plain
(hard) AC is *unsound*: it prunes boards that a licensed mismatch would make
perfectly legal. The exact per-colour filter is unsound there for the same
reason. On this project's engine the AC family is switched off in
break-tolerant runs. Piece-level all-different stays sound, since a break
never lets a piece be used twice; see
[Régin's filter](/research/build/reduce/alldiff-regin).

Switching propagation off is a design choice, not the only sound option. The
weighted and soft CSP literature exists for exactly this case: soft arc
consistency ([Larrosa & Schiex 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artint.2004.05.004))
and soft global constraints
([Petit, Régin & Bessière 2001](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-45578-7_31);
[van Hoeve, Pesant & Rousseau 2006](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10732-006-6550-4))
turn propagation into a sound *lower bound on the number of violations
still to come*. Here that could be a count of cells whose domain AC would
empty, or the per-colour supply deficit; a node whose bound exceeds the
remaining break budget can be pruned without losing any licensed board.
Whether such a bound pays for itself at Eternity II node rates has not been
measured.

## Verdict

For exact-matching search, arc consistency is the standard look-ahead and
removes prunings that forward checking leaves on the table. On this puzzle
the committed ablation found no score gain from AC over forward checking at a
60-second budget, and a plain row-major scan beat both on raw throughput. If
you build it, use per-colour support counters rather than generic AC-3 or
AC-2001, and first verify that propagation is where your cycles actually go.
For mismatch-tolerant record hunting, hard AC is unsound; a soft-AC lower
bound against the break budget is the sound alternative, and its value at
this speed is an open question.
