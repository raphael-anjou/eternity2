Approaches that look promising and did not move the score on Eternity II,
each with the strength of its evidence. None of these are bad ideas in
general. About half of the verdicts below are not yet established: of 43 entries,
10 are measured with committed data, 5 are reported by a named public source,
5 are argued from a derivation, 22 rest on runs whose data are not
committed, and 1 is measured but undecided. Only the first three groups are
called dead ends here; the fourth is listed separately, entry by entry, with
the experiment that would settle it, and the undecided one has its own short
section after it.

## Evidence grades

Every entry opens with one of four grades, and states the scope of its
verdict (board sizes, budgets, engines) where the evidence gives one.

- **Measured**: committed data in this repository back the verdict. The entry
  links the result file and names its reproduction command; the
  [run it yourself](/research/build/run-it-yourself) page has the setup.
- **Reported**: a named public source (a mailing-list message or a paper) says
  so, and the entry links it. Nothing is rerun here.
- **Argued**: the verdict follows from a derivation, given in the entry or on a
  linked page. It holds under the assumptions the entry states.
- **Claim without committed evidence**: a run is described, but its data are
  not in the repository and no public source reports it (a private report
  counts here too). These entries have the status *untested claim*, not dead
  end.

## Graded dead ends

These twenty verdicts rest on committed data, a named public source or a
derivation. Each holds within the scope it states, and no further.

### Symmetry breaking

*Assume the board has rotational or mirror symmetries and fix some pieces to
shrink the search.*

**Measured.** The official set was built with no rotationally-symmetric pieces
and no duplicates, and the single center clue pins orientation. There's no
global symmetry to break, so fixing corners just makes an arbitrary, not a
free, choice. [Why this is a wall →](/research/why/rare-color-geography)

The absence runs deeper than rotations and duplicates. One part is a claim without committed evidence, since the enumeration has no committed output: for color-relabeling symmetry, an exhaustive enumeration of every color permutation
that could map the piece set to itself (up to rotation) finds only the
identity, because each of the 22 non-grey colors has a unique adjacency
profile across the pieces. The symmetry group has size one; the generator broke
every color swap too. (The colors do carry one piece of structure worth
knowing: they fall into exactly three frequency classes. The 5 frame colors,
confined to the border band, appear 24 times each; the 17 interior colors split
into 5 appearing 48 times and 12 appearing 50, totalling 960 half-edges, twice
the 480 edges.)

Evidence: the rotation and duplicate checks are committed ([invariants.json](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/blob/main/research/topics/adversarial-piece-set/results/invariants.json): 1,024 distinct piece rotations, none fixed by a rotation; reproduction command on [why Eternity II is hard](/research/why/why-e2-is-hard)), and so is the color census ([color-geography.json](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/blob/main/research/topics/rare-color-geography/results/color-geography.json)). Scope: the official 256-piece set, exact.

### Check invariants on the cells you already placed

*Split the piece set by its color-relabeling structure and prune the search
with invariants computed over the part of the board already placed.*

**Argued**, by the construction argument that follows. In a backtracker that only
ever places matching pieces, the placed region always satisfies any constraint
that mentions placed cells alone: the search enforces those invariants by
construction, so a pruner that inspects what is already on the board never
fires. We tried three variants of the idea and none of them pruned a single
branch; there are no speedup or score numbers to report because the pruners literally never fired (that trial has no committed data; the argument does not need it). A spiral scan-order variant was additionally worse, not
better, since it lost the good early-region ordering. The scope worth keeping:
this refutes static invariants on the placed region under a matching-only
search, not every conceivable use of the piece set's color structure. Useful
pruning has to look at what could still go, forward checks over the candidates
for unplaced cells, or live inside a search that permits mismatches. Compare
the border-supply check further down: that one fires too late; these never
fire at all.

Evidence: the derivation is the first sentences of this entry; a search that never places a mismatch keeps every constraint over placed cells satisfied by construction. Scope: static invariants on the placed region in strict, matching-only search; not forward checks and not mismatch-tolerant search.

### Just throw more compute at it

*Run the same SAT or search solver on a bigger machine, a GPU, an FPGA, or
quantum hardware.*

**Reported**, for exhaustive search. The community ran this experiment at
scale: the eternity2.net grid put 1.6 teraflops and over $10^{19}$ CPU
operations on the puzzle in 2007 and 2008, then shut down without a solution
([msg 3511](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/3511)). Blackwood, who set
the standing record, reported that SAT solvers, GPUs and pre-solved 2×2
caches did not help him past it ([Records & solvers](/research/records)). The
reason is argued on [why a faster computer doesn't help](/research/why/prune-vs-speed):
a constant-factor speedup moves an exponential tree by a constant, while
pruning changes how fast it grows. The quantum hope has been re-floated
regularly, from [2007](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/1446) to
[2024](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/11407), and no one has run the
puzzle on quantum hardware or a simulator
([quantum approaches](/research/build/hardware/quantum)).

Scope: exhaustive search, on the hardware of the 2007 to 2008 grid. It does
not cover record hunting, where more speed buys more attempts, and it does not
cover a tuned GPU solver: measured GPU ports run tens to a hundred times
faster than one CPU core, no tuned full-board GPU solver has been published,
and whether a long GPU campaign would move the record is open
([GPU solving](/research/build/hardware/gpu-solving)).

### Tensor-network counting

*Treat the board as a tensor network and contract it to count or score
boundary-consistent colorings.*

**Argued.** It can only enforce that touching edges match, not that every
piece is used exactly once. That blind spot is enormous: the boundary-consistent colorings it counts
vastly outnumber real solutions, of which the first-moment estimate expects
about 14,702 with the starter clue and about one with all five
([solution counting](/research/build/analysis/solution-counting)). Local message passing simply can't see the global
one-piece-per-cell rule. [Why this is a wall →](/research/why/entropy-area-law)

One amendment, a claim without committed evidence (notebook runs, nothing committed): the blind spot can be patched, at
small scale. Adding a Lagrangian penalty per piece on top of the contraction
recovers uniqueness well enough to fully solve generated 4×4 (one second) and
6×6 (five minutes) puzzles, verified piece-unique and fully matched. The cost
wall arrives long before 16×16: the first full-scale attempt ran out of memory
around 3 GB and died, and the truncation needed to fit may discard too much
for the fix to keep meaning. That is a single attempt, so the patched variant
is unvalidated at full scale rather than proven impossible; the argued verdict above is about plain contraction. Enforcing uniqueness through multipliers
needs accurate inner marginals, and accuracy at 16×16's color count demands a
bond dimension whose memory and compute blow up.

Evidence: the argument is the first paragraph; a contraction over edge colors has no variable that counts how often a piece is used. Scope: plain contraction. What would settle the patched variant: a committed run of the Lagrangian-patched contraction on a ladder of generated boards (6×6 to 10×10), recording memory and solve rate.

### Solve the colors first, place pieces later

*Reformulate the board as a line graph over colors, solve for a
color-adjacency arrangement that is edge-consistent everywhere, then hope that
arrangement is easier to turn into a real tiling than solving for pieces
directly.*

**Reported.** It is a clean idea, explored on the list in 2023 to 2025: strip
the puzzle down to which color meets which, solve that smaller object, and use
it as a scaffold. The trouble is the smaller object is not small. Enumerating
just the interior color arrangements for E2 leaves about
$6.6\times10^{11}$ permutations, and 17×17 blows up to $2.97\times10^{13}$
([msg 11182](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/11182)), and every one of
those still has to be checked against a real piece assignment, because a
color-consistent arrangement need not be tileable by the actual 256 pieces at
all. It is the tensor-network blind spot in another costume: satisfying the
color adjacencies is necessary but nowhere near sufficient, and the
use-each-piece-once constraint that does the real work is exactly what the
color-only view drops. The author's own summary after the enumeration was that
you would "spend a lot of time just validating one" arrangement against all its
inner-color permutations. [Why this is a wall →](/research/why/entropy-area-law)

Evidence: the counts and the author's verdict are from [msg 11182](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/11182); nothing is rerun here. Scope: interior color arrangements as enumerated on the list in 2023 to 2025.

### Lattice reduction on the placement equations

*Encode placement as 0/1 integer equations, take the kernel lattice, reduce it
(LLL or BKZ), and search for the solution as a closest-vector problem: the
machinery behind the famous knapsack and integer-programming attacks.*

**Argued** (the core arguments are structural; the probe numbers below have no committed data and carry no weight in the verdict).
Three doors close in turn. First, the part a lattice attack handles cleanly,
each cell gets one piece and each piece one cell, is a totally unimodular
assignment system, solvable in polynomial time by the Hungarian algorithm; our
recovery experiments (10 of 10 planted 2×2 and 3×3 instances recovered
exactly, following the Aardal, Hurkens and Lenstra kernel-lattice
reformulation) demonstrate the method solving a problem that was never hard.
Second, the part that is hard, facing colors must agree, is a conditional
("bilinear") constraint with no faithful linear-equality embedding short of a
combinatorial blowup of auxiliary variables, which erases the lattice
advantage. Third, the color-supply system is not a knapsack: every coefficient
is exactly 1, so the classical low-density subset-sum attacks (Lagarias and
Odlyzko 1985; Coster and colleagues 1992) do not even apply, and an LLL attack
on the unit-weight embedding degenerates to plain enumeration. The scaling
probe agrees: exact-integer LLL on just the easy assignment kernel took 0.35 s
at 2×2 and 42.6 s at 3×3, and was killed past 175 s at 4×4 (kernel dimension
993); the full 16×16 backbone would be around 262,000 variables. (Those
timings are one pure-Python exact implementation, a practical statement rather
than an asymptotic one; the structural arguments carry the verdict.) Per-color
sublattices are cheap to reduce, under half a second, but 12 of the 60
shortest vectors examined at 3×3 already require one piece to sit in two
cells: they omit exactly the constraints that matter. The puzzle's hardness
lives in a problem shape, bilinear matching plus global distinctness, that
lattice-reduction tools are not native to; the sub-problem they are native to
is already polynomial.

Scope: the three structural arguments hold for any lattice attack on this placement encoding. The recovery counts and timings are a claim without committed evidence.

### Enumerate local clusters first

*List every valid 3×3 or 4×4 cluster, then stitch the clusters together into a
full board.*

**Reported.** The counts blow up before they help. In a run whose data are not committed, valid clusters around a single region already ran into the tens of millions, and
combining four corners reaches the order of $10^{12}$ piece-disjoint tuples.
You run out of time and disk long before the constraints prune anything. The
idea keeps getting rediscovered. In late 2024, 2×2 “macro pieces” came up
again, at around 4 million blocks before piece-disjointness even enters
([msg 11428](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/11428)), and veterans
pointed back at years of earlier 2×2 work in the archive
([msg 11429](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/11429)).
[Why this is a wall →](/research/why/rigidity-wall)

Evidence: [msg 11428](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/11428) (about 4 million 2×2 blocks before piece-disjointness) and [msg 11429](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/11429); the committed [reference numbers](/research/reference) count placements for small blocks only. Scope: 2×2 to 4×4 clusters stitched exactly.

### Widen the mismatch vocabulary

*Every solver in the record-holding backtracker family can leave at most one
mismatched edge per cell; by construction its index cannot even represent a
cell that breaks twice. Yet top community boards produced by local search do
contain four or five such double-break cells. So build the same search over
the wider space where a cell may charge two breaks, and hunt where no one else
can.*

**Measured**, at a short budget. In the committed [DFS study](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/dfs-study) ([report](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/blob/main/research/experiments/dfs-study/results/report.md), `just experiments dfs-study`), allowing up to two broken edges at one cell scored a mean of 428.5 matched edges (best 435) against 431.3 (best 435) for the one-break-per-cell ladder, over ten corner-pinned variants of the official puzzle at 60 seconds on one core. The longer paired A/B and the census below point the same way, but their data are not committed. A paired
A/B of 54 rounds, 27 seed-matched pairs across three schedule shapes and two
scan directions at 600 seconds × 4 threads per round, gave 20 ties, 4 wins for
the wider engine and 3 for the classic, every difference within ±3 matched
edges, with identical per-shape min/median/max distributions (415/420/425 in
both arms for one shape). The wider engine visited 0.87 to 0.94 times the
nodes for the same outcomes. A separate exhaustive census behind a 208-cell
prefix of a 464-scoring community board (five-clue scoring; context on the
[records page](/research/records)) found exactly four completions at seven or
fewer total mismatches: the 464 itself and three one-cell variants at 462 and
463, and zero of them used a double-break cell. The mechanism: the wider space
is explorable but never forced. With the mismatch budget spread over the whole
board the search almost never has two units of headroom left at one cell, so
it spends its budget the way the classic engine does; and in the near-perfect
endgame region the double-break stratum is empty outright. The known
double-break cells in community boards sit mid-board and were made by
local-search repair moves, not by any depth-first pass. Scope: an
equal-wall-clock tie for one engine family on the official puzzle, not a proof
that the wider stratum is empty everywhere; the starting hypothesis, that the
community plateau is a representability wall, is downgraded to an unsupported
conjecture, not refuted.

Scope of the measured part: 60 seconds on one core, ten corner-pinned variants, one seed, one engine family.

### Force a diverse border, then fill

*The border is a smaller sub-puzzle. Solve for many different valid frames
first, on the theory that variety in the frame seeds variety in the whole
board.*

**Measured**, for one producer. With one fixed break-tolerant beam (width 128), 28 random fully matched borders all completed to 434 to 441 of 480 (strict scoring, all five clues placed), and completing each border with several seeds shows no border effect: the spread between borders is no larger than the spread between seeds on one border (F = 1.24). See [the frame is not the basin](/research/why/frame-is-not-the-basin) (results committed, `just research-frame-is-not-the-basin`). A second committed measurement explains part of it: a perfect border does not guarantee a usable interior, since 180 of 500 perfect frames (36 percent) left at least one interior cell that no interior piece could fill; the same study shows the frame is not rigid either, since free exchanges of edge pieces revived 60 of 60 dead frames ([fluid frame](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/pipelines/fluid-frame), `just research-frame-manifold`). So this entry refutes border diversity as a score lever for one producer, not the frame as an object of study. A private report by William Millilaw, with no public record, describes the same outcome, in these terms. The
border is the easy part: from scratch it is already varied and quick to place.
The scarce resource is the interior fill, where the pieces run thin and the
mismatches concentrate. Forcing frame diversity spends effort where there is no
shortage and buys nothing where there is one. It is a recurring theme in this
project's own experiments too: the frame is worth surprisingly little
([STAGED](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/pipelines/staged) measures exactly how little), and
the endgame damage lands in the interior corners
([MOSAIC](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/pipelines/mosaic)).

Two further runs point the same way; their data are not committed, so their numbers are a claim without committed evidence. First, cataloguing
borders: a time-bounded search (120 seconds, explicitly not exhaustive; the
true count is unknown) produced 75,173 complete, internally valid border
rings. Feeding a 500-frame sample to the solver as pinned frames, 47 percent
exhausted the search in under 200 milliseconds under baseline propagation, and
essentially all were rejected outright once full propagation ran against the
official clue pieces. A ring that closes on itself satisfies only pairwise
edge-matching around the rim; joint feasibility with the clues and the
interior's use-each-piece-once constraint is a far stronger condition, and
essentially none of the catalogued rings meet it.

Second, perfect borders as seeds. We generated 5,000 distinct piece-unique
perfect borders by dynamic programming (all 60 border cells placed, every
border edge matched), sampled 100 evenly, and gave each the same pipeline: a
60-second constraint-propagation interior fill, then a 60-second local-search
polish. Top score of the whole batch: 436/480 matched edges; about half the
starts landed between 420 and 427, a quarter between 380 and 419, and none
above 437. The 455-to-460-class boards of the same era (strict scoring, all
five clues placed) all came from a depth-first producer that does not fix the
border first. The polish used a single seed and 60 seconds per start, so this
is a cheap-filter screen, not an exhaustive refutation: under a short
equal-budget polish, none of 100 border-first starts came within 20 edges of
what the direct search reaches. A perfect border over-commits the interior;
the fill then optimizes within whatever interior color profile the border
allows, and that family plateaus far below the best basins.

Scope: one interior producer whose ceiling sits near 440; whether a near-record producer would see a border effect is untested.

A later measurement on the real pieces with the five clues puts the cheap rejection of a frame at the four clue corners: a frame passes the four clue-block tests with probability about 2^-17.4, and the frames that do pass are not cheaply refuted (see [refute wrong frames cheaply](#refute-wrong-frames-cheaply) and [frame and corners](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/frame-and-corners)). The 47 percent and "essentially all" figures above are the uncommitted run's own and are not read against that measurement.

### Start from the center and grow outward

*The center has the most rotational freedom, so start there and let the
constraints accumulate as you grow toward the border.*

**Measured**, on the official board. In the committed [DFS study](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/dfs-study) ([report](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/blob/main/research/experiments/dfs-study/results/report.md)), a strict depth-first search that spirals from the center outward averaged 24.5 matched edges at 60 seconds on one core, against 376.6 for row-major order, over ten corner-pinned variants of the official puzzle. Border-first scored 66.6 in the same grid, so the measured lesson is that row-major beat both, not that border-first is good. On generated small puzzles, whose data are not committed, the comparison went the same way. Head-to-head on generated
puzzles from 5×5 through 8×8 against row-major and border-first orders,
center-out lost every one of the six configurations: typically 10 to 100 times
slower, it failed outright on four of the six within budgets where
border-first finished (one 5×5 stuck at depth 22 of 25 after 2 million nodes
on a puzzle border-first solved in 2,000 nodes), and it was about 60 times
slower on one it did solve; the 8×8 case was the worst of the three orders
tested. On those small puzzles, with one solver's scan orders, the direction and magnitude were uniform across all six cases. The mechanism:
freedom is the enemy of propagation. The border supplies hard, immediate
constraints (the gray edges); the center supplies none, so a center-out search
commits to arbitrary colors with no way to detect infeasibility early. More
freedom means less pruning, not faster search. Instead: the
[fill order](/research/build/backtracking/fill-order) page covers orders that
do help.

Scope: strict depth-first search, 60 seconds on one core, ten corner-pinned variants of the official puzzle. The small-board numbers above are the uncommitted run's own.

Center-out is one of the eighteen static fill orders later measured on the real pieces with the five clues; fifteen of the eighteen do not beat row scan and three are undecided (see [a static order beating row scan](#a-static-order-beating-row-scan) and [approaches priced out](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/approaches-priced-out)).

### Precompute 2×2 (or bigger) macro-tiles

*Solve every 2×2 block once, store the valid ones, and place four cells at a
time so the search is shorter.*

**Reported.** Markus Zajc worked through it in 2008: building the macro-tiles
just moves the work, it doesn't remove it. You trade a small set of single
pieces for a very large set of 2×2 blocks, so placing a block is faster but the
number of distinct partial boards is unchanged, still on the order of $10^{40}$
where it needs to be $10^{4}$. A 10× or 100× speedup on an out-of-reach tree is
still out of reach ([msg 5883](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/5883)).
Macro-tiles are a real constant-factor win for a fast solver, which is why the
[block-order playground](/playground/paths) supports them, they are just not a
domain reduction, and only a domain reduction would move the needle. The
ceiling this is measured against is the standing 470/480 record: Blackwood
reported pre-solved 2×2 caches did not help him past it
([Records & solvers](/research/records)). Instead: the one free lever that does
shrink the tree is the [fill order](/research/build/backtracking/fill-order).

Evidence: [msg 5883](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/5883) (Markus Zajc, 2008); not rerun here. Scope: 2×2 blocks as a domain reduction; as a constant-factor speedup they work.

### Border-supply pruning (the "lollypop" check)

*Before recursing, count the remaining border-colour demand against the pieces
still available; if supply can't meet demand, cut the branch early.*

**Reported.** Markus Zajc implemented this supply-versus-demand check as an
add-on to his constraint solver and benchmarked it on the 8×8: it cut about
0.0014% of tests while adding 8% to the runtime, a clear net loss. The reason
is instructive: on almost every branch the border-supply check would have
failed, the ordinary constraint propagation has *already* failed a step or two
earlier, so the extra bookkeeping pays for a cut the solver was about to make
for free ([msg 6060](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/6060)). A pruning
rule only helps if it fires before the checks you already run, not after.
Instead: the supply-versus-demand idea pays when it is folded into the
propagation that fires first, the
[all-different matching filter](/research/build/reduce/alldiff-regin).

Seventeen years later the same idea was re-run in this project's engine, across all colors instead of just the border; that run has no committed data, so its numbers are a claim without committed evidence: track how many piece edges of each color
remain unplaced, and cut the branch when a frontier cell demands a color whose
supply is exhausted. Official puzzle, single thread, one 10-second run: the
baseline visited 630 million nodes at 63 million nodes per second; with the
check, 388 million at 39 million, roughly a 40 percent throughput loss for the
same maximum depth (192) and the same best score (344 matched edges). Zero
additional pruning, and the reason is now precise: a candidate index keyed by
(top color, left color) already *is* this propagator. If placing a piece would
exhaust a color some upcoming cell demands, that cell's candidate bucket is
simply empty one step later and the search backtracks anyway; the explicit
check pays per node for a cut the index makes for free.

A dynamic per-color budget variant (also not committed) fared no better on a strict backtracker:
for each color, track how many of its half-edges are already permanently
mismatched, and prune any partial board that can no longer reach the target
score for that color. Across 15 deep snapshots of real search states (depths
195 to 212 of 256 cells) it fired zero times: a solver that never places a
mismatching piece only ever creates partials that cannot yet violate the
budget, and by the time the budget would bite, ordinary propagation has
already cut the branch. (On a mismatch-tolerant search the check is untested
here.) A related non-lesson: sorting all candidates globally by a color-rarity
weight, the same order at every cell, is not an informed heuristic at all; it
is equivalent to a fixed relabeling of the pieces and adds no information.

A related ring check is measured: on the generated hard ladder, adding a border-seam multiset balance check to a propagating solver left the node counts unchanged on all 50 calibration instances ([CALIBRATION.md](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/blob/main/research/experiments/hard-ladder/CALIBRATION.md), rows `scb-gacolor_ac3` and `scb-gacolor_ac3_ns1`). Scope: strict search; the reported numbers are for an 8×8 in 2008; mismatch-tolerant search is untested.

### Forbidden-pattern pruning during search

*We proved that a set of 2×2 piece combinations can never appear in any fully
matched board. The obvious next step: check every completed 2×2 patch during
the depth-first search and prune when it is forbidden.*

**Argued**: vacuous by construction. An empirical check (24 boards, 225 patches each, 5,400 completed patches, zero forbidden; data not committed) agrees with the argument. The forbidden-pattern test asks whether four
pieces can match internally under any rotations; but an edge-strict search
only ever completes a 2×2 after already matching all four internal edges, so
every patch it completes is feasible by construction and the check can never
fire. The theorem's discriminatory power is over piece subsets, which would
need a forward-checking integration that tests patches before their cells are
filled, and in a row-major scan the cheap version of that forward check is
already performed implicitly by the candidate index. The theorem itself is
still true and useful elsewhere: see
[forbidden patterns](/research/why/forbidden-patterns). Same closing moral as
the lollypop entry: a prune that fires after your existing checks is worth
exactly nothing.

Evidence: the argument is in this entry, and the theorem's counts are committed ([feasibility.json](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/blob/main/research/topics/forbidden-patterns/results/feasibility.json)). Scope: edge-strict search that completes a 2×2 only after matching its internal edges.

### Refute wrong frames cheaply

*Fix the 60 border pieces first and fill the interior second, on the theory that wrong frames die cheaply.*

**Measured**, on the real pieces with the five clues, independently re-checked. The search only pays if wrong frames cost less to refute than the search they save. On 60 frames that pass the four clue-block tests (the stratum that contains the true frame), cheap refutation of a full frame is dead: the lower median speed-up over plain search was 0.89, 0.12 and 1.00 (a matching check in rows order, in spiral order, and the Glucose SAT solver with the frame as assumptions) against a line of 100. SAT refutes a frame at once or not at all (46 frames within 0.35 s, the rest hit the 600 s cap), and plain search was censored on 25 of 60. Refuting an arc of the border does not break even either: for arcs of 44, 42, 40 and 38 border cells the nested cost is 27.7, 31.4, 35.4 and 39.5 bits against budgets of 25.5, 29.0, 32.9 and 37.0. The cheap information about a frame sits at the four clue corners, about 17.4 bits in all. Frames away from the corners are **undecided**: 37 far frames, 16 censored, median 75.12 bits with an interval of [42.64, inf]. The interior under a frame that passes the corners is a model figure of about 10^13.6 nodes in the mean, replacing an earlier 10^8.3.

Evidence: [frame-cores](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/frame-cores); the series page is [frame and corners](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/frame-and-corners). Scope: the real pieces with five clues and these refuters; no saving is claimed for a frame-first search, and results on the three generated control boards are toys, not Eternity II progress.

### A static order beating row scan

*Find a fixed fill order whose tree is smaller than the row scan's.*

**Measured**, on the official pieces with the five clues: eighteen static orders, each a population estimate of the full tree with five repetitions ([orders-and-pins](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/orders-and-pins), part A). Fifteen of the eighteen do not beat row scan: they are measured, or bounded below by a Markov bound, above row scan's 10^40.453. Three are **undecided**, the three annealed orders: the estimator's population dies at free depth 38 to 48 while row scan's dies at 191 to 198, so their estimates are lower bounds on the tree, not sizes, and the data show them neither cheaper nor dearer. The best line scan is row scan from the bottom; the worst, from the top right to left, is 1.10 bits above it. A separate analysis puts row scan within 0.24 bit of the best static order it could find, with no certificate that row scan is the best.

Evidence: [orders-and-pins](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/orders-and-pins); the series pages are [approaches priced out](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/approaches-priced-out) and [the price of a full search](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/price-of-a-full-search). Scope: static orders only; adaptive orders are priced on the first page, and the three annealed orders stay open.

### Late filters on the row scan

*Add a cheap check to the row scan that removes wrong boards before the tree grows.*

**Argued**, from arithmetic over a measured profile (the table is a sum over the level profile of the row-scan tree, recomputed by a second analysis; it is not an experiment). A filter that removes every wrong node from free depth d onward, for free and with no false cuts, saves at most 57.1 bits from depth 52, 29.8 from depth 80, 17.1 from depth 96, 6.8 from depth 128, 2.4 from depth 144 and 0.5 from depth 158. The checks tried bite late. On generated boards (toys), arc consistency run alone refutes 57 percent of sampled wrong prefixes at free depth 96 and 90 percent at 112 (a follow-up that was not independently checked), and its priced saving is about 5.5 bits of nodes while one check costs about 13 bits per node. A colour-census prune at depth 144 on one scan direction gains 1.02 bits [0.72, 1.27] against a ceiling of 2.35 there; with two scan directions the gain is -0.04, with four -1.88 (independently re-checked). A filter that paid would have to bite before depth 96 and cost almost nothing; none was found.

Evidence: [approach-pricing](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/approach-pricing) and [prefix-statistics](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/prefix-statistics); the series page is [late filters](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/late-filters). Scope: the two checks above, on generated boards; no filter that bites before depth 96 was built on the real pieces, and pruning at several depths at once was not measured.

### Semidefinite relaxation on small boards

*Relax the placement problem to a semidefinite program (one variable per tile, rotation and cell, lifted to a matrix) and read a solution, or a route to one, from it.*

**Measured**, on generated toy boards of 4×4 to 6×6, with a negative reading and some parts undecided. The lift has 478 placements at 5×5, 1,290 at 6×6 and 156,816 at 16×16. At 5×5 it allows only blends of true solutions on seeds 1, 2 and 4 (independently re-checked), is undecided on seed 0 (the largest-leak solve has not converged), and is not exact on seed 3, which has 403 solutions: about 0.042 per cell sits on ten placements that no solution uses (one solver family only). The three 6×6 runs did not converge, so no 6×6 reading exists. A 5×5 solve took 400 to 1,100 s on boards that depth-first search clears in at most 0.1 s. At 16×16 one dense copy of the matrix is 183 GiB and the factor plus nonnegativity multipliers about 119 GiB (independently re-checked). Claims made along the way that the lift is exact at 5×5, and odds that it is exact at larger sizes, were withdrawn.

Evidence: [relaxation-small-boards](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/relaxation-small-boards); the series page is [relaxations on small boards](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/relaxations-on-small-boards). Scope: toy boards, one solver family; a decomposition into local lifts, the only route that could make the convex form affordable, was not run. Nothing here is Eternity II progress.

### The low-rank form of the relaxation

*Replace the lifted matrix by a low-rank factor, cheap enough to run at larger sizes.*

**Measured**, on generated toy boards, negative. At 5×5, 200 of 200 runs solved on 15 boards at about 5,200 evaluations per solution (independently re-checked), but this is a randomised search with an exact acceptance test, not a solver of the relaxation: the same form with nonnegativity removed solved 46 of 50, and with a nonnegative factor 0 of 50 starts solved in each of four variants (not independently checked; the derivation was re-checked). Plain annealing with restarts is 8 to 35 times cheaper per solution at 5×5 (independently re-checked) and 3.6 to 63 times cheaper at 6×6 (one pass, intervals overlap on three boards); at 7×7 annealing found nothing in 210 runs, a rate below 26 percent per run, not shown to be zero. ### Small changes to a complete search engine

*Add a backjump rule, a value order, a matching check or a better instrument to a complete search with propagation, and shrink its tree.*

**Measured**, on generated boards of 6×6 to 9×9 (toys, never the Eternity II pieces), each preregistered and independently re-checked. The baseline is `bb-mrv`, a complete search that fills the most constrained cell first; a method passes only with a paired median node ratio of 0.5 or lower. Dropped:

- **Blocker backjump** (evict the cell that blocks the failed cell, with tabu) used 4.68 times the nodes of the stronger baseline `scb` at 6×6 (CI 2.40 to 6.55) and solved 4 of 30 at 7×7 against 16 of 30; a random-eviction twin solved 3 of 30. The kill covers this incomplete repair search, not conflict-directed backjumping with no-goods.
- **Value-impact order** (try values by the options their propagation removes) gave node ratios of 1.075 [0.779, 1.931], 0.829 [0.544, 1.316] and 1.096 [0.669, 1.871], and loses to a random order among the same probed options at 6×6 (1.297). The kill covers this impact measure only.
- **Hall test on top of the propagation** removes under 1 percent of nodes at the median pair (node ratio 0.992 to 0.999) while costing 2 to 5 percent in wall clock; propagation already enforces the size-one cases. An earlier 10 percent gain at 6×6 was measured against `scb` and does not survive `bb-mrv`.
- **Partial-match ladder** (an instrument for score at fixed work on planted boards) found 2 of 5 entrants in the 2 to 4 percent band it needed; dropped for this entrant pool and budget only, as an instrument, not as a verdict on a method.
- **MRV scaling pilot** (does the ratio to `scb` keep falling at 9×9?) was censored for both in 6 of 8 pairs because of the cap; it reads no evidence, not "the ratio stopped".

The one change that helped changed cost only: a representation-only rewrite makes a node 5.3 to 6.1 times cheaper with the node sequence unchanged (0 of 140 mismatches), about 2.6 bits (derived), and a faster node never shrinks the tree.

Evidence: [blocker-backjump](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/blocker-backjump), [value-impact-order](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/value-impact-order), [hall-on-mrv](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/hall-on-mrv), [partial-match-ladder](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/partial-match-ladder), [mrv-scaling-pilot](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/mrv-scaling-pilot); the series page is [search engine tracks](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/search-engine-tracks). Scope: 6×6 to 8×8 for the node ratios, one engine family; nothing on the 16×16.

### Keying and band experiments

*Remember dead ends, or key the search on a few colours or cut edges, so a branch is recognised as dead sooner.*

**Measured**, with three kills, each preregistered and independently re-checked.

- **Difference-map bands** (exact band projections added to a difference map, on generated boards): no instance solved at any width at 7×7 (0 of 30 for each width, against 23 of 30 for `bb-mrv`), so no growth exponent exists; at 6×6 the iteration ratios to width 0 have intervals [0.41, 1.36], [0.43, 1.30] and [0.77, +inf], all containing 1, and a width costs 3 to 33 times more per iteration. Killed for this method at this cap.
- **Cut-keyed replay** (propagation partitioned on two cut edges, replayed over the exact `bb-mrv` trees of 60 generated boards): the cut key keeps 0.486 [0.469, 0.500] of the nodes at 7×7, a random key off the cut line keeps 0.419, and the work is 1.211 times `bb-mrv`'s. The pruning comes from partitioning in general, not from the cut.
- **Five-clue keying** (680 one-ring keyings on the real pieces with the five clues): none of 2,240 runs placed a legal node at depth 201 or more, against 9,758 for the default keying on the same openings. Killed, but confounded: the default also differed in quota slack. A follow-up (**five-clue quota**) separated the two: with the same five clues, one-ring keyings asked for the same 79 percent quota share reached depth 201 on 14 of 16 openings for the published triple, while the default under a 147-of-150 quota reached it on 0 of 16. Quota slack, not the colour set, explained the earlier kill, and the surviving claim is narrow: no one-ring keying under the scaled quota works.

Evidence: [difference-map-bands](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/difference-map-bands), [cut-keyed-replay](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/cut-keyed-replay), [five-clue-keying](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/five-clue-keying), [five-clue-quota](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/five-clue-quota); the series page is [keying experiments](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/keying-experiments). Scope: 1e9 nodes per run on the real pieces, 6×6 to 8×8 on generated boards, no restarts and no cut key inside a row-scan solver.

## Stated but not established

These twenty-two entries describe runs whose data are not committed, or a
private report with no public record. Each may well be a dead end, but
nothing a reader can open shows it yet, so each carries the status *untested
claim*. The numbers inside them are the run's own, unverified. Each entry ends
with the experiment that would settle it.

### Survey propagation

*The message-passing method that cracked huge random SAT problems by finding
solution clusters.*

**Untested claim.** One generated 4×4 and three settings, with no committed data, plus a heuristic argument and a 2008 forum remark. It
assumes the problem looks locally like a tree. Eternity II is a grid with a
short cycle in every 2×2, which breaks that assumption, although loopy message
passing does work on some grid problems (LDPC decoding, vision models), so the
cycles alone prove nothing. In
practice the messages flatten out instead of sharpening, the opposite of what
makes it work on random SAT. We know of no published success for it on grid
edge-matching puzzles. The community noticed the flattening early: in 2008, experiments with
propagating piece probabilities found that constraint influence from the clues
and corners seems to “fade out” about two cells away
([msg 6208](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/6208)). Instead: the pruning
that does pay on this grid is exact, not probabilistic;
[arc consistency](/research/build/reduce/arc-consistency) and the
[all-different matching filter](/research/build/reduce/alldiff-regin).

We later ran the obvious rescue ourselves: message passing plus a per-piece
Lagrangian penalty meant to restore the use-once constraint, on a generated
4×4. It did not converge in that run. At least one piece's marginal
collapses to exactly zero everywhere, and once a marginal hits zero no
multiplier can revive it, so the duality gap never closes: the residual stayed
around 1.2 to 1.7 across more than 50 outer iterations in all three damping
and step-size settings we tried (minimum piece marginal 0.00 in every
configuration; maximum 2.2 to 2.3 where it should be 1). That is one toy
instance and three settings, already failing at 4×4. The contrast with the
tensor-network note below is the informative part: the identical Lagrangian
trick works when the inner step is an expensive exact contraction and fails
when it is cheap message passing. The accuracy of the inner marginals is the
whole game.

**What would settle it:** survey propagation and its Lagrangian rescue run on a committed ladder of generated boards (6×6 to 10×10 near the critical color count), recording marginal polarization and decimation success against a plain backtracker.

### Belief-propagation move ordering

*Run belief propagation over the colors and use its per-cell hints to decide
which piece to try first.*

**Untested claim.** No data for the race below are committed, and the linked page measures partner counts, not belief propagation. The hints come out almost uniform, so they barely rank the
candidates. When we raced it head-to-head, picking the next move at random did
as well or better, because a fixed informed order tends to repeat the same
mistakes. [Why this is a wall →](/research/why/no-forced-moves)

**What would settle it:** a committed race of belief-propagation value ordering against random and default orders on the generated hard ladder, scored on nodes to a first solution.

### Sort candidates by future flexibility

*Pre-sort each candidate list so pieces whose bottom and right edges open the
most options downstream get tried first: a move-ordering heuristic that costs
nothing at solve time.*

**Untested claim.** The runs below are not committed. On the official puzzle, single thread, 10 seconds × 3 runs
against a tuned baseline: same maximum depth (192), same best score (344
matched edges), same node-visit pattern, and throughput dropped from 84 or 85
million to 80 million nodes per second, about 5%. A multi-thread 30-second
spot check scored 429 against a 444 baseline, but that is a single seed: it
shows the ordering can perturb outcomes, not that it reliably hurts. The
mechanism: the deepest reachable point was set by the solver's mismatch
schedule, not by move order, and "future-flexible" pieces correlate with
popular pieces the existing ordering already tries early, so the sort adds no
information; it only reshuffles memory layout and pays for it in cache
locality. Scope: this is the verdict for one tuned schedule path on the
official puzzle; the picture could differ on schedules without a depth wall or
on generated puzzles with uniform color distributions. Same lesson as the
belief-propagation entry above, from a cheaper angle: informed static
orderings do not beat the defaults here.

**What would settle it:** the 10-second runs committed with their hardware, then repeated at several schedules and on generated puzzles with uniform color counts.

### Relaxation bounds

*Solve the linear-programming relaxation to get a tight ceiling on how many
edges a board can match.*

**Untested claim** for the bounds below: the LP ceiling near 478 and the 30-minute MIP run are notebook measurements with no committed data, as the [LP page](/research/build/exact/lp-relaxations) states. Only the 4×4 timing is reported, from the archive. The relaxation lets pieces be fractional and spread across
cells, which fakes matches that no real board can have. The result, for the
matched-edge LP with a strong board's border fixed (formulation on the
[LP page](/research/build/exact/lp-relaxations)), is a ceiling around 478,
still several edges above the best real boards on the
[records page](/research/records): a gap that certifies nothing. The binding constraint is global piece-uniqueness, which the
relaxation throws away. LP formulations were on the community's table in the
first summer, and already there a 4×4 took over an hour to solve
([msg 1678](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/1678)). Instead: the
[LP and ILP relaxations](/research/build/exact/lp-relaxations) page shows what
these encodings can still earn (impossibility proofs and near-optimal fills on
sub-boards), which is where their value actually is.

The integer version is no rescue at full scale either. With the 60 border
cells fixed, we asked an open-source MIP solver (HiGHS, 4 threads, a 30-minute
limit) for an integer-optimal interior: roughly 154,000 binary placement
variables and 13,000 constraint rows. It returned without finding any feasible
integer placement at all, and even mislabeled the empty assignment "optimal"
at objective 0, a reminder to sanity-check solver statuses. Scope that result
tightly: one solver, default settings, one budget. It does not refute integer
programming; we have since watched the encoding and solver choice change
everything on smaller instances (a CP-SAT-class solver full-solves instances
the same LP/MIP stack fails on, in under a second). A generic formulation at
full scale simply exceeds what one mainstream solver could even find feasible
in half an hour; restricted-region and restricted-corpus integer models do
work, and that is where these solvers earn their keep.

**What would settle it:** the conditional LP solve with a strong board's border fixed, and the MIP run with its solver log, committed so the ceiling and the solver status can be rechecked.

Semidefinite relaxations, a tighter family than the linear one above, were run on toy boards and are graded as their own entries below (see [relaxations on small boards](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/relaxations-on-small-boards)).

### Pattern databases (the Rubik's Cube heuristic)

*Precompute, for every small patch of cells, the minimum number of edge
corrections any completion needs, and sum those per-patch costs into an
admissible heuristic: the technique that cracked Rubik's Cube and the
15-puzzle.*

**Untested claim**, as a verdict on the method. What is established is trivial: at the empty board, any admissible bound returns 480 on a puzzle that has a solution. At the empty board, by direct computation, the bound
carries no information. Every potential edge can be matched on its own, so the
per-edge upper bound comes out at 480, the maximum, and the per-2×2-patch bound
(225 patches × 4 internal edges, corrected for overcounting) is also exactly
480. That is what any admissible bound must return on a puzzle that has a
solution by construction, so the root result is trivially true and says nothing
about search. The 22 non-grey colors split into 5 frame colors, confined to the
border band, and 17 interior colors, so the 60 border joins and the 420
interior joins draw on separate palettes; the root bound is 480 either way.
Pattern databases earn their keep at interior search nodes, given a placed
frontier and a depleted piece pool, where a patch bound over the remaining
pieces can fall below the trivial one and captures real interactions (each piece
once inside the patch, joint four-edge consistency). Patch databases indexed by
frontier colors and remaining pieces are untested here. Instead: the bounds
that already prune at interior nodes, such as the
[all-different matching filter](/research/build/reduce/alldiff-regin) and the
[LP and ILP relaxations](/research/build/exact/lp-relaxations), which encode
the global piece-once constraint.

**What would settle it:** patch databases indexed by frontier colors and remaining pieces, measured on node counts against the best baseline of the generated hard ladder.

### Learn a heuristic on small boards

*Train a neural network on small puzzles, then transfer it to the full 16×16
to guide the search.*

**Untested claim**: one model architecture, with no committed model, data or evaluation. We trained a model that nailed small boards and watched it
collapse on the real one. It learns from candidate moves filtered one way and
is then asked about moves filtered very differently, and the full puzzle's

across the size and color gap; other architectures are untested. [Why this is a wall →](/research/why/rigidity-wall)

**What would settle it:** a committed model, its training data and a transfer evaluation across board sizes and color counts, then a second architecture.

### Gradient descent on a soft board

*Make the board differentiable: a doubly stochastic piece-to-cell matrix by
Sinkhorn normalization plus per-cell rotation softmaxes, maximize expected
matched edges by gradient descent with temperature annealing, then round to a
real board.*

**Untested claim**: four seeds of one Sinkhorn schedule, no committed data. The relaxation gap was large
in every run. The soft score climbs to
367/480, but the best rounded board scores 336 matched edges (other seeds 324
and 325, best of 4), and rounding gets worse as the temperature sharpens; a
straight-through hard-forward variant is worse still at 280. The relaxation is
the Gumbel-Sinkhorn construction that
[Mena et al. (2018)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08665) applied to jigsaw and
permutation learning; this is one schedule of it, not the whole family. The strongest
test was also the smallest: restricted to the last two or three rows of a
459-out-of-480 board of ours (strict scoring, all five clues placed) with the
rest frozen, exactly where exact re-solving reliably earns one to three edges,
the optimizer could not even reproduce the board's own tail (41 versus the
incumbent's 49 region matches over 8 restarts on two rows; 63 versus 76 on
three rows). One campaign, but the gap on the easiest region is 15 to 20
percent below the incumbent, far outside noise. The mechanism echoes the
fake-480 and tensor-network entries on this page: the continuous optimum is a
superposition of many mutually incompatible boards, and the constraint the
relaxation softens, every piece used exactly once, is precisely the one that
carries the hardness, so softening it fakes matches no real permutation can
honor. It is a third failure mode in the learning family; see
[when learning collapses](/research/build/learning/when-learning-collapses).

**What would settle it:** the optimizer and its runs committed, then varied over schedules and rounding steps.

### Build the board one perfect row at a time

*Build row by row, using exact bipartite matching to pick the best possible
row given the edge colors the previous row exposes.*

**Untested claim.** A single deterministic run, not committed. Each row is locally optimal; the board still dies. In our test
the build hit a wall around row 10 of 16, at 294/480 matched edges, with rows
11 and beyond infeasible outright: the locally best early rows consume exactly
the pieces whose edge colors the later rows need, and by row 10 the remaining
pool can no longer supply the required colors at all. Keeping the 32 best
row-sequences in parallel does not help; all the leading candidates draw on
the same scarce pieces and starve together at the same row. (Single-run proof
of concept; the greedy dynamic program is essentially deterministic. Much
larger beams, 256 or 1,024, were not tried, though the shared-pool argument
predicts the same wall.) The one-line mechanism: local optimality does not
compose, because the binding resource is the shared piece pool and a
row-at-a-time commit spends it invisibly. What works instead is joint
foresight across rows: the full-board
[beam search](/research/build/construct/beam-search) our builders actually
use.

**What would settle it:** a committed run at beam widths 32, 256 and 1,024 recording where the build dies.

### Build from both ends and meet in the middle

*Grow the board top-down and bottom-up at the same time and join the halves at
a middle row: each half gets the easier, border-anchored part of the work.*

**Untested claim.** Two variants at one beam width, not committed. The join kills it. With independent top and bottom beams at
width 32, piece-disjointness alone failed on more than 99.9 percent of
pairings: 0 valid pairs out of 1,024. A second variant guaranteed disjointness
by re-running a dedicated bottom-up beam per top state, about 8 times the
compute, and still produced zero merges, because the meeting row demands an
exact 16-position color-sequence match on an alphabet of about 22 colors, and
with the remaining pieces constrained the matching set is effectively empty.
Two obstructions stack: independent searches pull from the same pool of
promising pieces, and even disjoint halves must agree on a full row of
interface colors that neither side optimized for. Building from both ends does
not remove the interface difficulty; it relocates the mid-board wall to the
meeting row. Scope: two variants at one beam width; softened joins (allow a
few interface mismatches then repair, or meet on a diagonal) were never run,
so the verdict covers the exact-join versions, not bidirectional construction
in general. The exact-methods cousin of this idea has its own page:
[meet in the middle](/research/build/exact/meet-in-the-middle).

**What would settle it:** the two exact-join variants committed, then a softened join (a few interface mismatches allowed, then repair) and a diagonal seam.

### Cube and conquer

*Split the SAT instance into millions of sub-cases (cubes), solve each
independently, and recombine. The technique that settled Schur number five and
the Pythagorean triples problem.*

**Untested claim.** The only account is a private report by William Millilaw (May 2026) with no public record a reader can open, and no public or committed source reports cube-and-conquer run at scale on the full puzzle ([SAT and CSP encodings](/research/build/exact/sat-csp-encodings)). The report says it does not clear the board. On 16×16 the cubing phase does not partition the instance
into anything tractable without heavy preprocessing, and the conquer step (a
lookahead solver on each cube) was itself slower than running kissat straight.
In that setup (one implementation, a lookahead cuber with kissat, one encoding
and one budget) the method worked up to about 8×8 and did not scale past it;
other cubers, encodings and budgets are untested. If the report holds, it joins the long line of exact methods that meet the same wall as
integer programming and SAT decision, without a foothold past it. Instead:
[which wall stops which method](/research/why/walls-and-methods) lines up every
exact attack against the barrier it dies on, so you can see why this one
is likely to stop there.

**What would settle it:** a published cube-and-conquer run on the full board (cuber, encoding, budget and solver logs), or on a ladder of generated sub-boards to locate where it stops scaling.

### Generate boards with a transformer

*Train a GPT-style model on a corpus of boards, condition it on a target score,
and have it emit fresh high-scoring boards the search never found.*

**Untested claim.** A private report by William Millilaw (May 2026), with no public record a reader can open, says each stage failed for its own reason. A 51-million-parameter model learned the grammar of a
board (each piece used once, 96 percent structurally valid) but not the physics:
unconditioned, its boards averaged around 250 matched edges, near random.
Conditioning on a target score raised the average, but every board it emitted at
the top of the range was an exact token-for-token copy of a memorized community
board, McGavin's 469 among them. With only a couple of dozen distinct elite
boards in the training set against tens of millions of parameters, the model had
simply memorized them; the score condition became an index lookup. A final
reinforcement-learning pass made it worse, not better, because the elite tail is
sampled far too rarely to give a stable gradient. The lesson matches the
[small-board transfer](/research/why/rigidity-wall) result on this page:
imitation learns the distribution it is shown and cannot invent the rare
structure that a record needs.

**What would settle it:** a published model, training corpus and sampling log, with duplicates in the corpus counted for the memorization test.

### Ant-colony construction (pheromone trails)

*A colony of agents each builds a full board greedily at random; a pheromone
field over (cell, piece, rotation) decisions is reinforced along the paths of
the best finishers (a standard max-min ant system: evaporation, elite
deposits, clipping), so future builds concentrate on globally consistent
regions.*

**Untested claim**: three failures described, no committed data. The standalone ceiling was 296/480 matched
edges, the best over the swept configurations (best configuration at one seed,
proof-of-concept scale), below even a plain wide-search baseline. Turning the
pheromone learning on made the colony worse: the population mean dropped from
about 265 to about 252 over 40 iterations while a no-pheromone random-restart
control held about 265 and reached 289; that control was a direct
head-to-head. And the learned field carries no information about what good
boards actually do: checked against a 455-scoring board of ours (matched
edges, all five clues placed), the field ranked the good board's choice first
in 1.2 percent of cells, and its mean percentile for that choice was 0.502
against 0.5 for a random ranking, statistically indistinguishable from chance, so it
cannot even serve as guidance for a better search. The mechanism is credit
assignment: with many legal candidates per cell and good final scores
arising from vast, interchangeable sets of early choices, "this decision was
on a good path" is noise. The pheromone amplifies an arbitrary early lottery
winner and the colony converges prematurely on a mediocre basin.

**What would settle it:** the colony, the swept configurations and several seeds per configuration committed, with the random-restart control.

### Monte-Carlo tree search with a cheap prior (the TSP transplant)

*A 2024 result showed that for the travelling-salesman problem, a strong MCTS
with a parameter-free prior matches learned neural heatmaps
([arXiv:2411.09238](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09238)). Port the underlying
engine ([Fu, Qiu and Zha, AAAI-21](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.10658))
faithfully, mapping its k-opt tour move to the exact assignment analogue, a
cyclic relocation of k pieces that is always feasible.*

**Untested claim**: two negatives described, no committed data. As a refiner: the pairwise-swap phase is a
sound repair engine (boards damaged from 452 matched edges down to 295 recover
to exactly 452) but never exceeds its starting score, and the headline
k-opt-style move is completely inert; across every run and the whole
hyperparameter grid (mixing weights, cycle length, simulations per move),
roughly 2,500 to 3,150 sampled relocation cycles per run produced zero
improvements. As a from-scratch producer: a constructive per-cell bandit
variant, after a 2.5 to 3.3× hot-path optimization to make the comparison
fair, performs like a beam of width 512 to 1,024, measurably below a
width-2048 beam at equal wall-clock (mean solve fraction 0.596 versus 0.743 on
our scaling ladder of generated puzzles; 0.644 versus 0.721 on the hard rungs,
24 runs), and the gap does not close with budget. The zero-improving-cycles
result is structural rather than a tuning failure, though only the faithful
port was tested. The mechanism: TSP k-opt works because the move chain follows
existing tour edges, so it reconnects mostly compatible structure by
construction; an edge-matching placement problem has no tour for the chain to
follow, and relocating a whole tile disturbs matches on all four sides at
once. The paper's transferable half, that search plus a cheap prior can
replace a learned model, held up; the search itself is TSP-shaped. On this
puzzle, raw [beam width](/research/build/construct/beam-search) remains the
better use of the same wall-clock.

**What would settle it:** the port, its hyperparameter grid and the ladder runs against the width-2048 beam at equal wall clock, committed.

### Recombine two good boards

*Take two high-scoring boards, keep the cells where they agree, and use
partition crossover to splice the disagreeing regions into a child at least as
good as both parents. The genetic operator with a tunneling guarantee on
pseudo-Boolean problems.*

**Untested claim.** A private report by William Millilaw, with no public record a reader can open, says he implemented it and benchmarked it on pairs of boards scoring 455 and above. It degenerates. The permutation constraint (a
piece cannot be reused) forces the disagreeing regions to merge into one
component the moment you close them under piece-uniqueness, so the operator
collapses into "pick the better parent" on more than 99 percent of pairs. The
rare improving splices topped out at 469, the score of the parents, never above.
It is the same lesson as the [rigidity wall](/research/why/rigidity-wall) from
the recombination side: the good boards all sit in one narrow basin, so mixing
them produces more of the same rather than something new.

The transplant version (one run, no committed data) failed the same way, from the other direction. We took a
442-matched-edges partial of ours and a finished 459 board (strict scoring,
all five clues placed) and grafted the donor's placements over the largest
region where the two disagreed, 71 cells: the score fell from 442 to 271.
Grafting cumulatively through the six largest regions never climbed back above
271 (241 to 271 across the six variants). Local repair on this puzzle
typically recovers five to ten edges; the graft digs a hole of about 170. One
partial and one donor, a decisive worked example rather than a universal
constant, but the mechanism is generic: a grafted region gains its donor's
internal matches and breaks edges all along its boundary with the untouched
cells, and a region's boundary is large relative to its interior, so boundary
damage dominates. Repairing it means re-choosing the cells just outside the
seam too, and iterating that requirement expands until transplanting a region
has become rebuilding half the board. Good boards do not decompose into
exchangeable parts.

**What would settle it:** partition crossover run on the public record boards, with the code and the pair-by-pair results published.

### Decode the mismatches like a corrupted codeword

*View a perfect board as a codeword, every internal edge a parity check, and a
high-scoring board as that codeword plus a small error syndrome; if the error
pattern has structure, syndrome decoding or belief propagation should localize
and clear it cheaply.*

**Untested claim**, from one 459-out-of-480 board of ours (strict scoring, all five clues placed) with no committed data. This entry and the next are two lenses that treat the mismatch pattern as structured signal, and both found none in that board. The syndrome is as
unstructured as it could be: the 21 mismatches involve about 21 distinct color
pairs (only two pairs repeat), split into 13 disconnected fragments, and every
color's total half-edge count is even, so the parity floor on mismatches is
zero; nothing algebraic forbids a perfect board or forces these errors.
Decoding accordingly buys nothing. Exactly re-solving the error-support cells
(34 scattered cells, warm-started) holds at 459 across 8 seeds and a
200-second solve, no better than the plain rectangular-band re-solve it was
raced against; growing the region one hop makes it too big to re-solve well
(433); and without a warm start the scattered region regresses to 448. One
board studied, though the mechanism suggests the picture is generic: decoding
shortcuts require a structured error (repeated collisions, a low-dimensional
coset, one repairable cluster), and a maximally spread syndrome whose
fragments couple only through the piece inventory is exactly the regime where
decoding degenerates into the same exhaustive tail search we already run. The
mismatches are a budget allocated globally, not a local error to invert.

**What would settle it:** the board and the re-solve runs committed, then repeated on several boards, including the public record boards.

### Treat the mismatches as physical defects

*Model each mismatch as a charged topological defect in the color field:
defects should sit at conservation-forced, minimum-interaction sites, and
opposite charges should annihilate.*

**Untested claim**, same board as the previous entry, no committed data; every premise failed there. One part is measured elsewhere on public boards: the mismatches of record boards sit in one band of rows set by the fill direction ([where the mismatches live](/research/why/mismatch-geometry)).
Position predicts mismatch location extremely well: the row index alone
separates broken from unbroken edges with an AUC of 0.895, and all 21
mismatches sit in the last-filled rows. Every color signal is noise:
color-rarity AUCs of 0.46 to 0.50, and the mismatches carry common colors, not
rare ones. The color-conservation floor on the tail is about zero; the frozen
top pushes down no color demand the remaining pieces cannot supply, and even
the sharpest orientation-aware matching bound forces only 0 to 2 mismatches
where 10 to 19 exist. The charge spectrum has essentially no opposite-sign
pairs, so there is nothing to annihilate. A defect-repulsion objective added
to the exact tail re-solve changes which tie-broken solution comes out but not
the ceiling; the last row is provably optimal given the rows above it. (A
cold-start region-solve sweep was stopped after a single seed, at 441, once
the verdict was clear; treat that number as a single-seed illustration.) The
finding worth keeping: the mismatches are not defects in a color field at all.
They are pure combinatorial orientation-frustration from piece exhaustion in
whatever region is filled last; the color bookkeeping balances, and what
breaks is the joint four-sided constraint over all remaining pieces at once.
No single-cut, per-color, or pairwise-energy reduction captures it. The
geometry of where breaks land has its own page:
[mismatch geometry](/research/why/mismatch-geometry).

**What would settle it:** the board, the AUC computation and the sweep committed, then repeated on the public record boards.

### Build a fake 480 and repair it

*Start from a board with every edge matched by allowing duplicate pieces, then
greedily swap in the real pieces one at a time, hoping to keep the score at
480.*

**Untested claim.** The only source is a private report with no public record a reader can open. William Millilaw ran it and it collapses into whack-a-mole: every
real piece you force in breaks edges elsewhere, and the repair never converges,
topping out around 463. The reason is the lesson underneath this whole page.
The barrier to 480 is not that the edges are hard to match; a fake board with
repeated pieces matches all of them easily. The barrier is the global
constraint that each of the 256 pieces is used exactly once, and that is exactly
what the fake-480 construction throws away. The wall is informational, not a
matter of local edge repair. Instead: the
[rigidity wall](/research/why/rigidity-wall) is why local repair cannot cross
this last gap, whatever board you start it from.

**What would settle it:** a published run of duplicate-piece repair with its schedule and its score trace.

### Seed the repair search with an exact solver's deep prefix

*Use an exact constraint-propagation backtracker to build a very deep
consistent prefix, pin it, and hand it to the local-repair search as a head
start.*

**Untested claim**: one seed, one schedule and one repair preset, no committed data. The committed [repair study](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/repair-study/findings) points the other way for a different kind of start: a start built by a break-tolerant depth-first search was the highest-scoring variant in that study, at a mean of 446. The pinning trick
genuinely deepens the exact search itself; between rounds it took the solver
from depth 27 to depth 152, score 23 to 297 matched edges, a real depth
amplifier, and that positive half remains useful when hunting exact solutions
rather than high scores (see the
[backtracking pages](/research/build/backtracking)). But as a score-seeker it
backfires: repair from the pinned 297/480 partial (184 cells fixed) finished
at 424/480 matched edges, versus a 430-to-450 median (best 451) for plain
cold-start repair in the same-era 16-seed comparison batch. That seeded arm
was a single seed, one schedule and one repair preset, so read it as "this
configuration underperformed every plain cold start in the comparison batch",
not as a refutation of all exact-prefix seeding. The mechanism is the same
start-from-the-wrong-object lesson as the fake-480 entry above: an exact
solver in first-solution mode optimizes consistency, not score. It returns the
first feasible deep prefix it finds, not a good one; "deep and consistent" is
not "close to good", and pinning makes the mistake permanent, since the repair
search cannot undo cells it inherited and would never have built that skeleton
itself.

**What would settle it:** exact-prefix seeding run inside the committed repair-study harness, with several seeds and the same comparison batch.

### Encode the human strategies

*Frame first, compact region growth around the clues, most-constrained-cell
first, disciplined backtracking, and deliberate "spend your mismatches wisely"
steering: code up how the best human solvers think.*

**Untested claim**: six seeds per order, no committed data. The committed [DFS study](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/dfs-study) compares related static orders on the official board (row-major, spirals in and out, border-first) and row-major wins there too. A perfect frame is found in
milliseconds and buys no traction (none of the five clues touch the border).
The real killer is piece exhaustion, and it is order-invariant: along a build,
the mean number of unused pieces that fit a cell perfectly collapses from 3.5
in the early rows through 2.6, 2.0, 1.6 and 1.4 to 0.4 by the final row, so a
mismatch is nearly always forced at the end, wherever the end is. Three human
fill orders were raced at 6 seeds each (matched edges): row-major 392,
center-out spiral 382, clue-anchored growth 359. Every order dumps its
mismatches on whatever it fills last (the spiral on the outer ring;
clue-anchored smeared everywhere, and scored worst), and a "preserve common
colors for later" tie-break made every order worse. A single guided trajectory
tops out around 392, roughly 67 edges below what a wide beam reaches on the
same hardware. The mechanism: the deficit that produces mismatches is a
conserved, global inventory fact; ordering chooses which cells inherit it,
never whether it exists. And the one human faculty that would actually help,
tracking thousands of hypotheses in parallel with global lookahead, is exactly
what [beam search](/research/build/construct/beam-search) already is. See also
[why there are no forced moves](/research/why/no-forced-moves).

**What would settle it:** the three human fill orders and the piece-exhaustion trace committed in the DFS-study harness.

### Drive the backtracker in concentric rings

*Drive the scheduled backtracker of the record-holding family inward in
concentric rings, on the theory that a layered geometry matches where the
breaks want to be.*

**Untested claim**: one schedule paired with one ordering, one run per budget, no committed data. A related committed result: [CAS](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/pipelines/cas), which solves the board ring by ring inward from a perfect border, plateaus at 429 to 437 of 480. The run described here hit a depth wall that more time did not move. A
one-hour run on the official puzzle visited 3.4 billion nodes with the deepest
placement stuck at a wall of depth 80: the wall was at 80 after five minutes
and gained one cell over the rest of the hour. A two-hour run finished at the
same 382/480 matched edges as the five-minute run. One schedule paired with
one ordering, one run per budget, so the verdict is that this pairing fails,
not that layered orders are refuted in general. The mechanism: the scheduled
solver's relaxation plan, where it is allowed to spend its mismatches, is
tuned for a row-by-row scan; an externally imposed ring geometry fights the
schedule instead of helping it, so the search jams at a fixed depth regardless
of budget. [Fill order](/research/build/backtracking/fill-order) matters, but
it has to be co-designed with the mismatch schedule, not bolted on.

**What would settle it:** the ring-order runs committed, then a mismatch schedule designed for the ring order, compared with row-major at equal budget.

### Share a transposition table across parallel workers

*Chess engines share a transposition table between threads; do the same here,
hashing each explored frontier so no worker re-explores a subtree another
worker already exhausted.*

**Untested claim**: one measurement run of eight workers, not committed; the build was cancelled on its strength. Eight workers ran 15 seconds each on
the official puzzle with an identical solver, only the candidate order
shuffled per worker: each worker placed about 214 cells at its deepest board
(maximum depths 192 to 235 across the eight), but the pairwise agreement
between any two workers' deepest boards averaged 1.8 cells (maximum 5), an
agreement fraction of 0.8 percent. One measurement run of eight workers, but
against the 50-plus percent a shared table would need to pay, the effect size
leaves no ambiguity. The mechanism: the search is extremely path-dependent;
the first piece tried at depth k reshapes what is available at depth k+1,
compounding down the whole trajectory, so differently ordered workers never
reconverge on the same partial board. A dedupe table only pays if paths
reconverge the way chess openings funnel into shared middlegames, and this
search has no such funnel. The flip side is good news: parallel workers
genuinely cover different territory, which is why simple uncoordinated
parallelism scales (eight threads reached depth 235 and 427 matched edges in
30 seconds where one thread reached 192 and 344);
[distributed solving](/research/build/faster/distributed-solving) builds on
exactly that.

**What would settle it:** the eight-worker overlap measurement committed and repeated with several seeds and longer runs.

### Eulerian border-closure check

*A valid border ring must trace an Eulerian cycle in the graph whose vertices
are border-edge colors and whose edges are border pieces: a clean necessary
condition proposed on the mailing list back in 2007. Check it during search
and prune borders that can no longer close.*

**Untested claim** for the pruning test, whose data are not committed. The structural fact behind it is measured: the five frame colors form a connected multigraph in which every color has degree 24, so a full ring is Eulerian ([ring purity](/research/why/ring-purity), [ring_purity.json](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/blob/main/research/topics/ring-purity/results/ring_purity.json)). The test described found zero pruning power on the official puzzle. Tested at five search
depths with 200,000 trials each, a million partial borders in all: the
Eulerian condition never fired on any configuration that ordinary propagation
had not already killed. The condition is necessary but vacuous on the actual
piece set, and the reason is a design fact: the official set was generated
with its rare colors placed only on the border ring, two per border piece, and
that structure makes Eulerian closure automatic for any border partial that
gets anywhere near a full ring. The configurations the check would reject die
much earlier under standard arc-consistency-style propagation. Same family
lesson as the lollypop check above: a prune only helps if it fires before the
checks you already run.

**What would settle it:** the single-core engine already has a border Eulerian propagator; run it on the generated hard ladder against the same solver without it, and commit the node counts.

One question has committed data and no verdict. It is not a dead end.

### Clause learning against propagation

*Add clause learning to a solver and let it remember why branches died.*

**Measured, verdict undecided**, on generated framed toy boards of 5×5 to 7×7 (ten per size, one thread, 3,500 s limit), independently re-checked. Work is counted in conflicts for the learning solvers and in nodes or branches for the propagation-only ones, as bits of log2 (row-scan nodes over work). Paired on the same static order, learning on against off gains **1.31** bits at 5×5 (95% 1.06 to 1.49) and **2.03** bits at 6×6 (1.33 to 2.26). There is no 7×7 value: the learning arm finished 0 of 10 boards, and the 7×7 table entries computed from runs cut at the limit are upper bounds, not measurements. The stop line fixed in advance (the lead over the propagation-only control must rise by 2 bits from 6×6 to 7×7 and by 3 bits from depth 104 to 88) is **undecidable on this data**: the depth clause needs a real-piece part that was never run, and the size clause allows a rise of at most 2.09 against a threshold of 2, with no lower bound. A fault found by the re-check, a dropped 7×7 board that CaDiCaL finished in 430 s and that is the learning solver's best case, means the first note's figure bounds only the 8 boards it used. Size is confounded with tightness (4, 5 and 7 inner colours). Priced for the full puzzle at **0 to 2 bits in time** (the re-check's estimate); in seconds, learning is about 3 bits slower at 6×6.

This is not a closed dead end and is not graded as one. It tells nothing about learning at 8×8, on real-piece prefixes, with a cardinality solver, or past 3,500 s.

**What would settle it:** the real-piece part (40 wrong prefixes at free depths 112 to 88), an 8×8 rung, and runs past 3,500 s, with a propagation-only control.

Evidence: [learning-vs-propagation](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/learning-vs-propagation); the series page is [learning against propagation](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/learning-against-propagation). Nothing here is Eternity II progress.

## What the established dead ends have in common

Two lessons recur among the measured, reported and argued entries. A
relaxation that drops the use-each-piece-once constraint fakes matches that
no real board can honor: tensor-network counting and the colors-first view
both fail there. And a prune helps only if it fires before the checks a solver
already runs: invariants on placed cells, border supply and forbidden patterns
all fire after the candidate index has already cut the branch. Both lessons
are scoped. They are about strict, matching-only search, while the record
engines tolerate mismatches, and they say nothing about faster hardware for
record hunting, which is open
([GPU solving](/research/build/hardware/gpu-solving)).

The row-scan series adds a scoped third lesson. The tree is thin before the depth where the tests that refute wrong boards act, so a check that bites only after about depth 96 has a ceiling of 17.1 bits and one that bites after depth 128 of 6.8 ([late filters](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/full-solve/late-filters)); the largest gain priced anywhere in the series is about 5.5 bits of nodes, against a gap of 56 to 73 bits.

The second section is the larger one. Treat each of its entries as a
hypothesis with a stated way to test it, not as a closed door. For the open
directions, see the [open problems board](/research/open-problems), and for
the argument about exhaustive search,
[why a faster computer doesn't help](/research/why/prune-vs-speed).
