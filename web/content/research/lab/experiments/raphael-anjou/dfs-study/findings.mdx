Three comparisons carry the [DFS study](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/dfs-study).
Each isolates one decision by holding everything else fixed. All scores are the
mean matched-edge count over the ten corner-pinned variants, single core, sixty
seconds. Throughput is search-nodes per second and is never compared across
families.

## What low-level specialisation buys: throughput, and only that

The two baselines run the *same* strict row-major algorithm: `NAIVE-CLEAN` as a
readable general engine, `NAIVE-CODEGEN` as a hand-specialised, row-major-only
16×16 hot loop. The specialisation delivers what it should on the axis it targets.
`NAIVE-CODEGEN` is faster per node, median 44.9 against 32.4 million nodes per
second; the per-instance gain is not reliable, because the grid ran six jobs at once
and the codegen rates split into two clusters with no instance-related cause. On
*score* the codegen twin is slightly lower on nine of the ten instances (by one to
ten points): specialisation did not buy score here, a small consistent gap rather
than a large harm. At a fixed wall-clock a faster engine reaches a
different point of the same tree, and a backtracker's best partial does not move
monotonically with how fast it got there. The baselines are therefore a clean
*speed* comparison and are deliberately not presented as a *score* comparison. The
score effects worth studying are all on the axes below, where the search itself
changes.

## Fill order: row-major wins, and the wrong order is catastrophic

Fix the engine (strict, no heuristics) and change only the order cells are filled.
The six orders differ only in where the search sends its frontier, shown below.

<Figure title="The six fill orders, traced as the path the search walks">
  <PathOrderDiagram />
</Figure>

- **Row-major** is the strong baseline, averaging 377. Its damage zone stays
  constant, since every new cell has the same two placed neighbours, so it reaches
  deep before the strict wall.
- **A strict border-first or spiral fill collapses.** Filling the border ring
  first, with no look-ahead, closes the 60-cell frame and then stalls a few cells
  into the interior (depth 66 to 77, mean score 67): the first frame it commits to
  apparently admits no interior completion it can reach, and chronological backtracking
- **Bottom-up row-major is erratic on this clue geometry** (mean 226, from 366
  down to 18 depending on the instance), because the pinned clues sit in rows the
  bottom-up fill reaches early and cannot satisfy.

Same engine, same sixty seconds, a swing of more than 300 points from the fill
order alone. This is the measured form of a piece of community wisdom: border-first
is only good *with* a heuristic to choose cells within the ring. On its own it is
one of the worst orders available.

## Heuristics: MRV rescues border-first, but throughput craters

Now fix the path near the frame and add one thing at a time. The largest lever is
**MRV**, filling the most-constrained empty cell next, chosen dynamically. It
turns the stalled border-first (mean 67) into a search that averages 324 (best
341) at depth 190 or so. It also recomputes the most-constrained cell over the
whole frontier at every step, so in the committed runs node throughput falls by
nearly four orders of magnitude, from tens of millions of nodes per second to
6,000 to 8,000. Far fewer nodes are visited, and each one goes further. Node rate
and score are different axes.

Adding heavier look-ahead on top did **not** buy more score at this budget. Every
run uses one seed and is deterministic, so the only variation is across the ten
shared instances, and the comparisons below are paired per instance.

- **Forward-checking** (reject a placement that empties a neighbour's domain)
  averaged 322, two or three points *below* plain MRV on nine of ten instances.
- **Arc-consistency** and the **per-colour supply check** averaged 321 each, and
  returned identical scores on all ten instances. Against forward-checking the
  paired differences are mixed (forward-checking higher on seven instances, lower
  on one by fifteen points), so no propagator leads here.
- A **rare-colour-first** value ordering was inert, identical to plain MRV on nine
  of ten instances, echoing the community's repeated negative result on
  within-bucket value orders.

The lesson is not that propagation is useless. It is that at a small fixed budget,
on these instances and with this implementation's MRV cost, no extra reasoning
recovered its per-node cost within sixty seconds.

## Breaks: past the wall, and the schedule is the lever

Strict backtracking, whatever its order or heuristic, hits a wall well short of a
full board: row-major tops out around depth 208 of 256, and even the fastest
strict variant (NAIVE-CODEGEN) only reaches 216. The record engines get past it
by *breaking*: allowing a bounded number of interior edge mismatches,
released on a depth schedule, with a rule that no cell may carry too many broken
edges. The score of a full board is then `480 − #breaks`.

- **Breaks clear the wall.** A depth-gated break budget reaches past depth 245 and
  averages the low 430s (break-1 means 431 of 480, best 435), a large gain over the
  strict high-370s on the same instances and budget. This is the mechanism behind
  the community's record backtrackers, not a different paradigm but a depth-gated
  relaxation of the matching rule.
- **Allowing a second break per cell did not help here.** One-break and two-break
  reach the same best board (435), and their means (431.3 against 428.5) sit within
  the two-break variant's own spread, so neither leads on average. What does
  separate them is consistency: the one-break variant is tightly clustered (never
  below 427), while the two-break variant ranges down to 402. The double-break
  geometry the community's 460-boards use appears to need more than sixty seconds
  to pay off; at this budget the extra freedom mostly widens the branching without
  reaching better boards. This is a null result, reported as measured rather than
  the gain one might expect.
- **The schedule is a lever, but its effect here is bimodal.** Verhaard's slip
  ladder unlocks breaks earlier than Blackwood's (depth 193 against 201) and scores
  lower on average (mean 399 against 431). The mean hides two outcomes: on four of
  the ten instances it matches Blackwood's ladder (428 to 430, using nine or ten
  breaks), and on the other six it stalls near 380 having committed only two or
  three breaks, with most of its break budget unspent. Why it stalls on those six
  is not established. When and how fast breaks open is a tuning decision rather
  than a detail.

A naming note: VERHAARD-SLIP here is a break backtracker run on Verhaard's slip
schedule. It is a different algorithm from the swap-annealing "Verhaard
reimplementation" on the [engines page](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/engines),
and the two scores do not cross-check each other. The break bookkeeping here is
checked by the canonical rescorer, which recounts every returned board.

## Where the community engines sit: two grids

Blackwood and McGavin are the high end of this same family, and both build and run
on this machine, so this study ran them on both a pinned and an unpinned grid, with
every score canonically rescored from the engine's own board. The result is a
finding in its own right, and it has two halves.

**On the pinned grid, they collapse.** McGavin's C, built with its author's own ARM
flags (native tuning plus link-time optimisation), reaches depth 205 to 211 on the
unpinned and official-clue instances (it counts tiles placed, a different event from
our search nodes, so its rate is not compared with ours here), yet pinning three
corners collapses it to depth 21, a canonical score of 13. Its generated scan path
never visits the corners early, so a pinned corner constrains its neighbourhood at
once and dead-ends the fixed path almost immediately. Blackwood's C# hardcodes its
piece set and scan, so it cannot even express an arbitrary corner pin; on the
matching five-clue constraint its break heuristic thrashes within the first few
dozen cells (the committed records give 34 and 47 cells, a canonical score of 75),
because it is tuned for the near-unconstrained one-clue instance where its record
was set.

<Figure title="The corner-pin collapse: cells placed out of 256">
  <PinCollapseDiagram />
</Figure>

That collapse is the point. A record engine built around one clue configuration
does not transfer to another, and a fixed scan path cannot absorb an arbitrary pin.
Our from-scratch engines treat a pin as a pre-placed cell the scan simply skips,
which is why they, rather than the community binaries, are the stand-ins on the
pinned grid.

**On a fair unpinned grid, they run as designed.** Give every engine the official
pieces with only the one mandatory centre clue, and nothing dead-ends on a corner.
In sixty seconds, single-threaded, McGavin reaches about 390 (the committed record
gives both 385 and 392 for this run), our strongest break engine 344, the Verhaard
reimplementation 286, and Blackwood 214. Each row is in effect one deterministic run:
the three seeds return identical scores, so they are not replicates. Read those as
what a single thread buys in a minute from a cold start, not as any engine's
ceiling: the community's record boards (see [records](/research/records)) came from
far longer runs on many cores, which this budget cannot show. What the grid does
show is that all four run properly once the pins that break a fixed scan path are
gone, which is exactly what the pinned grid denied the two foreign engines. Both
grids are on the study's leaderboard above.

## The through-line

One theme runs through all three comparisons: **at a fixed sixty-second budget, what
the search is allowed to do mattered more than how fast it did it.** The fastest
strict variant per node (the codegen baseline) scores slightly *below* plain
row-major, lower on nine of the ten instances by up to ten points, and the slowest
strict variants (MRV with propagation, 6,000 to 8,000 nodes per second) score about
55 points below it. The only variants that beat row-major are the break variants,
which run at a few million nodes per second with no propagation and win by changing
*which* branch is legal: a relaxation of the matching rule, not smarter per-node
reasoning. This is one budget, one machine and one seed per instance. It does not
show that throughput is irrelevant, only that within this grid the constraint
mattered more than the speed.
