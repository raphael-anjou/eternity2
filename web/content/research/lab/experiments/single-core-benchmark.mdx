Give every solver the same budget, one thread and one minute, and which one
wins? Several solvers, ours and our implementations of the community's two
record backtrackers, each ran once on ten corner-pinned variants of the official
puzzle, single-threaded, 60 seconds a run. Every board was re-scored by one
canonical scorer; no engine's self-reported score is trusted. The maximum
possible score is 480.

<Callout kind="caution" title="Every engine here is our code">
  `blackwood_style` and `verhaard_style` are **our from-scratch
  implementations** of Joshua Blackwood's and Louis Verhaard's published
  algorithms, not the authors' own programs. Verhaard's `eii` only ever shipped
  as a Win32 binary with no source, so the grid runs a reconstruction (its
  quota tables recovered from `eii.exe`, its slip schedule from his posts).
  Blackwood's real C# *is*
  [public](https://github.com/jblackwood345/EternityII_Solver), but it hardcodes
  its 256 pieces and its thread count, so it cannot read this grid's variants or
  be held to one thread without editing it. Scores here measure our reading of
  each algorithm, not the authors' engineering, and should not be quoted as
  "Blackwood scores N".
</Callout>

The leaderboard below shows the methods that compete on score. A second family,
the CSP presets (one arc-consistency engine run under a dozen ordering knobs),
is a different category: its best preset reaches about 183, less than half a
contender's score, so it earns no leaderboard row. That preset sweep is a study
in its own right, on its [own page](/research/lab/experiments/single-core-benchmark/csp-presets).

## What a minute of one thread buys

The two record backtrackers dominate this budget. Our Verhaard-style engine
reaches a mean of **440.8** (best 451) and our Blackwood-style engine **436.4**
(best 440), exploring 24 and 41 million search-nodes per second. Nothing else
comes close: naive DFS lands at **365.6** at about 274 thousand nodes per
second, and the best CSP preset at about **183**.

On the rows published here, the two fast backtrackers win by a wide margin,
but score does not follow throughput within them: the Blackwood-style engine
runs faster than the Verhaard-style one and scores slightly lower (the two are
not separated at n=10). Naive DFS and the two best CSP presets run at the same speed (274
to 281 thousand nodes per second) and still finish about 180 points apart, so at
equal throughput the shape of the search decides, and here the plain row scan
wins. The original grid also ran a beam producer and an ALNS engine; their rows
are withheld until those engines are published, so this page draws no
conclusion from them. Whether a slower but better-shaped search can beat the
fast backtrackers at this budget is therefore open on this page.

What this grid cannot tell you is where the ceiling is. The best board here (451)
is well short of the strict five-clue record on the
[records page](/research/records); the records were not set in a minute, they
came from farms and months. This measures per-thread efficiency at a fixed small
budget, nothing more.

## Reading the table

The two backtrackers are the top of this board, but their order is not settled.
The Verhaard-style engine spans 437 to 451 across the ten variants, the
Blackwood-style engine 431 to 440. Verhaard-style is ahead on 8 of 10 variants,
yet its mean lead (4.4 points, paired bootstrap 95% interval 0.8 to 8.5) comes
mostly from two runs at 451; the medians are 438.5 and 436.5, and the interval
on the median gap (−0.5 to 9) includes zero. With one seed per variant, the
spread across variants measures sensitivity to the instance, not run-to-run
noise, which this grid does not measure. Read ranks 1 and 2 as not separated at
n = 10. (The intervals come from
[`contender_stats.py`](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/single-core-benchmark/scripts),
run on the committed results.) The naive baseline is the floor: a fast,
junk-filled 365 that shows what raw matched-edge count looks like without any
board quality.

Throughput units differ by family and are never cross-compared. The backtrackers
count search-nodes per second. The CSP presets never beat naive DFS on any
variant: the best CSP score anywhere is 349, and naive DFS's worst is 359. The
strongest presets run at naive-DFS speed (about 280 thousand nodes per second);
the presets that run full arc-consistency drop to 5 to 11 thousand nodes per
second and score lower still.

The runs were not fully isolated. The grid ran six single-thread jobs at once on
an M1, which has four performance and four efficiency cores, and macOS does not
let a process pin a thread to a core. Some runs therefore shared or landed on
efficiency cores while held to a wall-clock budget, so throughput and score
carry a core-type confound. A rerun with at most four jobs at a time and several
seeds per variant would remove it.

## What each contender is doing

- **Verhaard and Blackwood (backtrackers, ranks 1 to 2).** Depth-gated-break DFS
  at 24 to 41 million nodes per second. They reach 431 to 451, and neither
  completes a board edge-perfectly inside 60 seconds; the endgame needs far more
  compute. Blackwood found his 470 after about a month on a single PC, and called
  it "a stroke of luck"
  ([message 10194](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/10194)).
- **Naive DFS (the baseline).** Fills the whole board allowing every break. In
  row-major order (`anjou-naive_rowmajor`) it reaches a high matched-edge count
  (365) but a low-quality board full of breaks. Fast, and junk. It sits on the
  board as a floor: the number a method has to clear to be worth
  anything. (The visit-order sensitivity of naive DFS, and why the spiral variant
  collapses to 78, is on the [CSP presets page](/research/lab/experiments/single-core-benchmark/csp-presets)
  alongside the other same-engine ablations.)

## Where these numbers sit

This grid caps every engine at one thread for 60 seconds, far below the compute
that produced the records. It measures per-thread efficiency and heuristic
quality, not peak reachable score. An engine that ranks high here reaches good
boards cheaply; the record numbers need many cores times hours.

Every variant here pins all 5 official clues (plus 3 corners, so 8 hints in
total), so the relevant ceiling is the **strict five-clue record**, which the
leaderboard's dashed line marks. Blackwood's 470 is a looser ladder: it respects
only the mandatory centre clue (1 of 5). The [records page](/research/records)
keeps the two regimes and their current numbers.

| reference | score | conditions |
|:--|--:|:--|
| Community strict five-clue record | [records](/research/records) | the same 5 clues these variants pin, without the 3 extra corners |
| This grid's best (Verhaard-style) | 451 | one thread, 60 s (mean 440.8 over 10 variants) |
| Blackwood-style in this grid | 440 | one thread, 60 s (mean 436.4 over 10 variants) |
| Community starter-only record | [records](/research/records) | Blackwood, ~1 month on a single PC; respects only the mandatory centre clue (1 of 5), a looser regime than these variants |

## Method and reproducibility

Each of the ten variants is the official puzzle plus three pinned corner cells
(distinct corner-piece arrangements), so all ten share the 256-piece set and 5
clue hints but differ in three corner constraints. They are emitted as both
site-schema JSON (for the native engines) and CSV (for the standalone engines)
from one generator, so every algorithm sees identical instances. One run per
puzzle, fixed seed; the corner arrangement is the only diversity axis. Every run
emits a bucas `.url`, and the score is the canonical matched-edge count from the
same scorer, never the engine's self-report. There were zero failures across all
150 runs.

Every engine on this page is our own open-source code and its source is in this
repository, including the two written from the community's published algorithms;
no third-party solver is vendored here. The leaderboard above shows the three
published rows that compete on score, and the
[CSP presets page](/research/lab/experiments/single-core-benchmark/csp-presets)
shows the rest. The crate workspace, the ten variants, the committed per-run
results and the grid scripts all live under the experiment's
[backing directory](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/experiments/single-core-benchmark).
`cargo build --release --manifest-path engine/Cargo.toml` there builds every
binary, and `just experiments single-core-benchmark` then reruns the grid (the
recipe itself builds only the native binary, so build the workspace first). The
native family (the naive and CSP presets) is one binary selected by preset; the
two backtrackers are a binary each, driven by a small wrapper. Both speak the
same puzzle-in, bucas-url-out contract, and every board is re-scored by the one
canonical scorer.

## Companion findings

Profiling put 96.6 percent of the CSP family's time in one heavily pre-optimised
AC-3 loop, so the benchmark speeds are that engine's real speeds, not an
implementation gap. Separately, the community's Blackwood constants do not
transfer across colour labelings as published: in a three-seed comparison on our
labeling, his privileged colours `{13, 16, 10}` scored 387 against 435 for the
re-fitted `{22, 21, 20}` that our Blackwood now uses, while every structural
element of the published spec is kept. The re-fit was chosen on that same
comparison, so 435 is an in-sample figure; a check on held-out variants has not
been run.

## Open work

The grid runs our implementations. All three of the community's record engines
can in principle be run directly, and that is the obvious next measurement:

- **Peter McGavin's C generator.** He posted the source to the list in January
  2026 as `genbody71.zip`
  ([message 11749](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/11749)). It builds on
  Apple silicon with `clang` and its generate pass emits a 10,363-line `body.c`
  specialised to one puzzle. It is the fastest single-core CPU backtracker the
  community has measured, at 295M placements/s on Joe's CPU
  ([message 11750](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/11750)), and it is
  absent from this grid.
- **Joshua Blackwood's C#.** [Public and
  GPL-3.0](https://github.com/jblackwood345/EternityII_Solver); it builds
  unmodified on .NET 8. But it hardcodes all 256 pieces in `Util.cs`, fixes
  `number_virtual_cores = 64`, and takes no arguments, so pinning it to one core
  or feeding it this grid's variants means editing his source, at which point
  the artifact is no longer purely his. Running it as published, on the plain
  puzzle, is the faithful form of that measurement.
- **Louis Verhaard's `eii`** ships as a Win32 binary with no source. It has not
  been run for this grid; running it under Wine or in a Windows/x86 virtual
  machine would give the ground truth against which our reconstruction could be
  validated.

None of these engines is vendored into this repository; they are fetched and run
locally when measured.
