Hard search problems have a difficulty knob. Loosen it and there are many
solutions, so a search trips over one fast. Tighten it and there are none,
which is often easy to prove. In between sits a narrow band where solutions
are scarce but real, and that's where search blows up. People call it a phase
transition, like water freezing.

For edge-matching puzzles the knob is the number of colors. Too few and pieces
fit together countless ways; too many and they barely fit at all. For a
196-piece interior, the colour count at which the expected number of
solutions of a random piece set drops to about one is close to 17. For random
constraint problems the hardest instances for known algorithms cluster near
such thresholds, but where exactly depends on the algorithm, and this
first-moment point is only an estimate: it overestimates the true
satisfiability threshold, and the transitions tied to algorithmic hardness
(clustering, condensation, freezing) sit below it
([Mézard, Parisi & Zecchina 2002](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1073287);
[Krzakala et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0703685104);
[Achlioptas & Coja-Oghlan 2008](https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.2122)). The
[published analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10601-012-9128-9)
measured an empirical peak for specific SAT and CSP solvers on generated
edge-matching puzzles near this setting. Eternity II uses 17.

The community had that number within weeks of launch: in August 2007,
Brendan Owen derived $I = (196! \cdot 4^{196})^{1/392} \approx 17.14$
interior colours from the "about one expected solution" criterion, exactly
the published parameters, years before the academic analyses
([Design the hardest puzzle, msg 1947](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/1947)).
That is a simplified heuristic aimed at roughly one solution. The refined
estimates are different: about 14,702 expected solutions with the mandatory
centre clue, and about one with all five clues, both ensemble averages over
random puzzles with the same piece statistics, not counts of this puzzle's
solutions (see [solution counting](/research/build/analysis/solution-counting)).

We measured this here. Owen's criterion was only ever stated for the full board,
so we ran it through a second size to see whether it holds. The same formula
predicts $(36! \cdot 4^{36})^{1/72} \approx 7.56$ interior colours for an 8x8, and
an 8x8 is small enough that a plain backtracker actually solves random instances,
so its difficulty curve can be swept directly. Sweeping solver effort against
interior-colour count on generated 8x8 boards (the site's own generator and DFS,
30 seeds per colour count, a 30-million-node budget), the search never solves a
board at 6, 7 or 8 interior colours, close to the size-8 criterion of 7.56. The
curve is not a clean single peak, though: the same DFS also fails on more than
half of the boards at 1 to 3 and at 5 interior colours (11 to 14 solved out of
30), solves 27 of 30 at 4 and 23 of 30 at 9, and every board from 10 colours
up. So the sweep shows a hard band near the prediction, located to within a
colour or two, rather than a sharp peak. On 16x16, with a 20-million-node
budget, the DFS solves nothing at any colour count from 8 to 17, so this sweep
cannot locate a peak; it only shows the median depth reached falling from 238
to about 200 of 256 cells as colours rise. The
[hardness-peak experiment](https://github.com/raphael-anjou/eternity2/tree/main/research/topics/hardness-peak)
holds the code, both sweeps, and the criterion recomputed in-repo (it reproduces
Owen's 17.14 at size 16).

## The transition, measured on real pieces

The colour-count argument says *where* the peak is. In March 2008 Brendan Owen
went and watched a phase transition happen, directly, on the actual pieces. He
took a thin 2-by-L interior rectangle and tiled it with random sets of E2's
interior pieces, four hundred random sets at each length, and recorded how many
could be completed with no mismatch
([msg 4909](https://groups.io/g/eternity2/message/4909)).

<RectangleTransitionChart />

The shape is the phase transition in miniature. A 2-by-1 or 2-by-2 strip is so
short that random pieces often just fit: 58% solvable at length 1. Then it dies
completely. From length 4 through 15, not one random set out of four hundred
tiles the rectangle at any length: solutions are so scarce they effectively do
not exist. And then they come back. At length 16 one set in four hundred works,
by 19 it is 8.5%, by 21 it is 57%, and by 22 nearly nine in ten. The region
where solutions are vanishingly rare but not yet impossible is exactly the hard
band, here seen as a count rather than a model. What the count measures is
satisfiability: how often a random set of 2L pieces can tile a 2-by-L strip at
all. It follows the first-moment estimate closely: the expected number of
tilings, $(2L)! \cdot 4^{2L} / 17^{3L-2}$, crosses one between L = 19 and
L = 20, against an observed 50% point near L = 21. It is a satisfiability
threshold, not a measurement of search effort (no solver work was recorded),
and the strip length of a 2-row rectangle does not map directly onto the
14x14 interior.

## Why it matters

This is the clearest single sign that Eternity II's parameters were chosen
with difficulty in mind. Board size, piece count and color split sit near the
point where a random puzzle of this size is expected to have about one
solution, which is where hard instances for known algorithms tend to cluster.
That is consistent with deliberate tuning; it is not proof that the puzzle sits
at the worst case for every search, since where the hardest instances lie
depends on the algorithm.

How do we know the peak is real and not just a story? Two ways meet here. The
published analysis
[derives it](https://repositori.udl.cat/server/api/core/bitstreams/0b6533fe-54e5-4070-85fe-80f7d35837d8/content):
for framed edge-matching puzzles, the color count where you'd expect about one
solution falls near 17, and their SAT and CSP solvers found their hardest
generated instances nearby. And you can watch a piece of it yourself in the
demo above: build real puzzles, count the work, and see it change with the
colour count. The impact is concrete but needs two cases kept apart. For exact
exhaustive search, a constant-factor speedup cannot make the full 16x16 tree
feasible. For stochastic record hunting, more throughput means more attempts
and can plausibly raise the best score, with diminishing returns. A full
solution most likely needs a better idea, not only more effort.
