Fill the board top-left to bottom-right and every new cell already knows two
of its colors: the north color from the piece above, the west color from the
piece to the left. The cell needs an unused piece that can show that exact
pair. Those demands are scarce, with only about three possible pieces on
average, and 47 of them have just one.

So a board that looks healthy, most pieces still in the box, can already be
doomed. The single piece that could serve a demand was used somewhere else,
and the moment the north and west neighbours of a cell create that demand,
the cell has nothing to place. In constraint-programming terms this is an
ordinary domain wipe-out: the cell's domain, given its placed neighbours, is
empty.

The scarcity is generic, not a feature of this puzzle. 196 pieces in four
rotations give 784 oriented placements spread over $17^2 = 289$ possible
(north, west) pairs, so about 2.7 pieces per pair is what counting alone
predicts. On 100 randomly painted 16x16 boards with the same palette (5 frame
colours, 17 interior colours, the engine's framed generator), the occurring
demands range from 261 to 282, single-server demands from 38 to 63 (median
51), and the mean from 2.77 to 3.00 servers. Eternity II's 269, 47 and 2.90
sit inside that range: typical, not extreme.

## Why it matters

A simple supply check, do the remaining pieces still cover the remaining
colour counts, does not catch this. Globally the supply is fine; the failure
is one scarce piece already used, not a shortage. The standard tools do catch
it. Forward checking detects the wipe-out the moment the second neighbour is
placed. All-different filtering in the style of Régin, run over the
partially constrained open cells, can detect some cases earlier, when two
cells already depend on the same last server. Nogood learning can remember
the conflict for later branches. What makes the failure costly is timing:
a cell's (north, west) demand does not exist until both of its neighbours
are placed, so the dead end is created by the most recent choices and is
often discovered only one step before it bites.

Set this beside [no forced moves from a single edge](/research/why/no-forced-moves)
and the picture is complete. Each piece fits next to dozens of others, so a
single edge gives the solver no hint of where a scarce piece should be saved.
Each interior piece can serve up to four demands (one per rotation), and some
of those demands have no other server, so spending the piece on one of them
can starve another. Freedom to place, little guidance on what to save.
