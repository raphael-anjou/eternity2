For one stretch of this project I put the solvers down and asked a different
question: not "what score can I reach" but "what can I prove". The plan was a
sweep. Take the puzzle into every branch of mathematics that plausibly has
something to say about it (flows and cuts, extremal combinatorics, statistical
physics, algebra, CSP theory, automata, proof complexity, the complexity of
local search) and push each branch until it either yields a theorem or explains
exactly why it cannot. The sweep produced thirteen families of results and
claims. This page is the map: a few sentences per family, with links to the
full write-ups where they exist.

The families do not share one standing, and each is tagged. **Committed**
means a proof or exact computation with code and results in the repository.
**Measured** means a real measurement with a stated fit or sample.
**Conjectured** means the write-up itself frames it as open. **Claimed** means
a statement from the research notebook whose proof or code is not yet
published here; treat those as announcements, not results.

One note on flavor before the list. Some of these results are laws the piece
set obeys, some are impossibility statements about the score ladder, and some
are negatives: arguments that a standard tool from another field, applied
correctly, certifies nothing here. Knowing that a door is locked, and why, is
how you stop paying rent on it, provided the lock has actually been checked.

## Laws the piece set obeys

**Ring purity (committed).** The five colors that appear only on border
pieces have $5\times24=120$ half-edges, which exactly fill the 120
ring-facing slots of the 60-piece border. In every solution each edge piece
points its single non-frame color inward, and the border problem becomes an
Eulerian circuit in a 5-vertex, 60-edge directed multigraph. The zero slack
is automatic for any puzzle cut from a painted solved board with a separate
frame palette, so it is structure, not a design extreme. Full write-up:
[ring purity](/research/why/ring-purity).

**Frame-ring counting (committed).** This family prices what the ring law
buys. Color matching alone cuts the first border placement from 59 candidates
to about 21, and a greedy color-matching ring build sits near $10^{-27}$ on
the probability scale, 53 orders of magnitude above a uniform ordering
($10^{-80}$). The BEST theorem counts the directed Euler circuits of the ring
exactly (about $3.5\times10^{41}$), and sampling places the number of valid
frame placements, corners included, near $10^{37.6}$. Each frame color closes
exactly 12 ring joints, and the 56 inward-facing edges carry a color demand,
fixed by the piece set alone, that every solution must reproduce. The numbers
are in the [ring purity](/research/why/ring-purity) topic.

**Flux invariants (committed).** Give every color a numeric weight and every
piece the vector of its east-west and south-north weight differences; summed
over any region, this telescopes to a boundary flux, and a quarter turn acts
on the vector as multiplication by $i$. Decomposing along the four characters
of the rotation group yields the linear piece-intrinsic invariants: a color
census, a Gaussian-integer flux law of full rank 22 on the real piece set,
and a checkerboard parity coupling rotation to cell color. Full write-up:
[flux invariants](/research/why/flux-invariants).

**A quasi-random piece set (partly committed).** Against random solved boards
painted with the same color counts, the real set has no rotation-symmetric
piece (as about half of painted sets do not) and 5 same-multiset twin pairs
(painted sets average about 6.6), but zero rotation-duplicate pairs, which
only about 1.4% of painted sets achieve; that comparison is committed with
the [piece-set page](/research/why/why-e2-is-hard). The wider audit of
second-order statistics (color-pair frequencies, adjacency matrices, the
spectrum of the induced transition graph) and a reconstruction theorem
(recovering the hidden layout from the bag is as hard as solving the puzzle)
are claimed, not yet published.

## Where the score ladder closes

**The parity floor: 479 is impossible (committed).** In any legal placement,
each color's one-sided appearances come in pairs. A single mismatch would
leave two colors odd, so no board scores 479: the ladder jumps from 478 to
480. This holds for every puzzle cut from a painted board. The minimum nonzero
defect is 2, realized by swapping near-twin pieces, and every solution has
about 150 one-move boards at that defect (125 near-twin swaps and 29 turns in
place, a floor, not a ceiling). Full write-up:
[the parity defect floor](/research/why/parity-defect-floor).

## Measured laws and conjectures

**The annealed landscape and the 470 plateau (conjectured).** Treat boards
uncorrelated with the built-in solution as a random ensemble and count them
by score with a naive independent-edge first moment: the count falls
smoothly, about 3 decades per point near the top, and crosses 1 at 480. The
curve has no feature at 470 and on its own predicts about $10^{18}$
uncorrelated boards at 475, so it does not explain the plateau. The overlap
gap that would explain it is a stated conjecture; the small-instance
enumeration that motivated it is not committed. The community's best boards
and their conventions live on [the records page](/research/records). Full
write-up: [the 470 plateau](/research/why/the-470-wall).

**Entropy and distinct pieces (measured).** The puzzle has two rules: edges
must match, and each piece is used once. Under the first rule alone,
colour-valid patches grow exponentially with their size. Adding the second,
the share of those patches that use distinct pieces falls below one in a
thousand near 44 cells (counted exactly to 3×3, sampled beyond on patches
four cells wide), faster than any fixed area law. That says the second rule
removes most locally valid patches; it does not show that the first rule is
easy, since edge matching alone is NP-complete in general. Full write-up:
[entropy and the use-once rule](/research/why/entropy-area-law).

## Machinery claimed not to compress it

**CSP width (claimed).** The plain 16 by 16 grid has treewidth exactly 16,
which sounds exploitable until the global all-different constraint over all
256 cells enters and makes every width-based tractability argument vacuous.
The propagation picture reported with it: plain arc consistency reduces 48 of
196 interior domains, while matching-based global consistency reduces 191.
Write-up not yet published.

**Convex relaxations (claimed).** The standard SDP lift and an LP relaxation,
built on small planted sub-instances with known optima, are reported to
certify only the trivial ceiling and to miss obstructions that elementary
counting resolves. Write-up not yet published.

**Algebraic certificates (claimed).** In the bounded-degree Nullstellensatz
over GF(2), the counting propagation a good solver already does is reported
to have degree-2 certificates, while refuting a piece-reuse defect in a
$K\times K$ window needs degree growing with the area. The tensor-network
alternative is reported to fail on rank: the per-cell tensor has effective
bond dimension near 289 with no spectral gap to truncate against. Write-up
not yet published.

**No lossless compression of the frontier (claimed).** An exact dynamic
program over scan frontiers compresses only if two distinct used-piece sets
can safely merge, and a Myhill-Nerode argument is reported to show they never
can, under any scan order; the exact top-row frontier on the real instance
grows by about 8.9 per column. Meet-in-the-middle splits face a complementary
problem: both halves draw from the same finite piece pool, so no sound
interface signature short of literal equality is known. Write-up not yet
published.

## The complexity claims

**Local search (claimed).** For a generalized palette, finding a local
optimum of the natural move set is reported to be PLS-complete in the worst
case, and deciding whether a specific better board is reachable by improving
moves alone PSPACE-complete; the bounded-palette version is a conjecture,
and the proofs are not yet published. This concerns the time to reach some
local optimum across a family of instances, not the score of local optima on
this one fixed instance, so it does not by itself explain the 470 plateau.

**The proof complexity of exhaustion (claimed).** A chronological
backtracker's closed "no completion exists here" subtrees are treelike
resolution refutations. General resolution, which clause-learning solvers
with restarts polynomially simulate
([Pipatsrisawat & Darwiche 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artint.2010.10.002)),
can be exponentially more compact than treelike resolution on some formula
families ([Ben-Sasson, Impagliazzo & Wigderson 2004](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00493-004-0036-5)),
so this argument does not rule out large gains from clause learning. Whether
E2-like families have resolution-width lower bounds, which would bound
general resolution too, is an unpublished claim; for one fixed finite
instance, asymptotic statements do not apply directly.

## What the sweep changes

The point was never to lower expectations. A perfect board exists by
construction, and nothing in these thirteen families touches that fact. What
the sweep does is sort statements by how firmly they are known: a few laws
are committed and checkable, some quantities are measured, and several of the
most quoted barriers are still claims or conjectures that need their proofs
published before they can carry weight. Every route that remains open comes
with a clearer statement of what is and is not known about it, and that is a
better starting point for the next attempt than a blank map.
