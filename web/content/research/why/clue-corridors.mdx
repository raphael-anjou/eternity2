The five clues are the only cells of the official puzzle whose contents you
know for certain. Stare at the board for a minute and an idea offers itself:
connect them. Lay a short chain of pieces from clue to clue, let each chain
become a fixed spine, and hope the spines constrain everything that comes
after. The idea was worth measuring, and the measurement is negative. Counting
shows that a corridor adds no constraint a different fill order would not also
pay; it only changes when the closing checks arrive. And in one controlled A/B
with a greedy filler, every arm that laid corridors early finished below the
same filler without them, at every board size tested.

## Five islands, and the bridges between them

The clues sit at cells 34, 45, 135, 210 and 221, that is (x, y) = (2, 2),
(13, 2), (7, 8), (2, 13) and (13, 13): four inset two cells from the corners,
one just left of centre. Their pairwise Manhattan distances run from 10 to
22. No two clues are close to adjacent: two clues at distance 10 have nine
cells between them, so the shortest possible bridge is a chain of nine
placements.

What a chain placement is worth depends on how many sides it has to match.
With $n = 196$ interior pieces and $C = 17$ colours on those pieces, a cell
matching $k$ already-placed neighbours has an expected
$b_k = 4n / C^k$ legal candidates: $b_1 \approx 46.1$, $b_2 \approx 2.71$,
$b_3 \approx 0.16$. A placement only prunes, in expectation, once $b_k$
drops below 1, which requires $k \ge 3$ matched sides. A 1-wide corridor is
built from $k = 1$ placements: each new piece touches only its predecessor,
and inherits about 46 legal candidates at every step, until the last piece,
which must also match the far clue.

## What one corridor is worth, against a baseline

The branching table is a uniform model, so the checker also counts exactly on
the real piece set. The real per-side supply agrees with the model: the number
of (piece, rotation) pairs presenting a given interior colour on a given side
averages 46.1 (range 43 to 49). Build the transfer matrix $T[a][b]$ counting
inner pieces that show colour $a$ on one side and colour $b$ on the opposite
side, raise it to the corridor length, and the matrix powers give exact walk
counts. For the nine-cell corridor between the closest clue pair, with both
endpoint colours fixed by the clues, that count is $5.6\times10^{13}$ walks
(the mean over colour pairs). Walks may reuse a piece; the mean-field
distinctness correction $\prod_{j=0}^{8}(1 - j/196) = 0.830$ leaves about
$4.7\times10^{13}$ fillings using distinct pieces.

That number sounds large, but it only means something next to a baseline.
Nine distinct interior pieces in four rotations can be laid in
$P(196, 9)\cdot 4^9 \approx 9.3\times10^{25}$ ways, so the corridor keeps a
fraction of about $5\times10^{-13}$ of them: thirteen orders of magnitude of
exclusion, exactly the $1/17$ per matched edge that any other edge check
gives. A nine-cell chain laid with one contact per cell anywhere else, such as
the start of a row-major scan, has a comparable count (about
$9.5\times10^{14}$ walks with the far end left free). So the count alone does
not separate a corridor from any other chain.

What does separate it is scheduling. As the
[constraint-immediacy page](/research/why/constraint-immediacy) shows, every
fill order pays exactly the same 480 edge checks. A corridor's cells are each
checked against one neighbour, like any first row, but its closing checks are
deferred: the cells beside the corridor arrive later with three or four
contacts, and a bad corridor piece is only refuted there (fail-last). Its
pieces are also drawn from the pool before any $k \ge 3$ cell exists. Route
two or three clue pairs and 9 to 20 pieces are spent that way.

## The A/B: every corridor arm scored lower

The counting says a corridor buys nothing over another order; an experiment
is needed to show that it costs. The study's original run compared three arms
that differ in nothing but the corridor phase: a control (clues pinned, then a
max-contact greedy fill with restarts), a 1-wide corridor arm, and a 2-wide
ribbon arm, each laying its clue-to-clue routes before the identical fill. It
ran on scaled generated instances with faithful clue geometry, at board sizes
N = 8 to 16, with 12 paired seeds per cell and 20 seconds per single-core run.

| Board | Control mean | 1-wide corridor | 2-wide ribbon |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 8×8 | 92.50 | -4.67 | -3.00 |
| 10×10 | 144.17 | -9.58 | -5.50 |
| 12×12 | 209.83 | -13.58 | -6.75 |
| 14×14 | 285.67 | -12.00 | -7.83 |
| 16×16 | 377.17 | -17.67 | -8.08 |

Scores are matched edges, and each delta is an arm against its own control on
paired seeds. Every corridor arm scored below its control at every size, with
paired Wilcoxon $|z| \ge 2.80$. The harm grows as the boards, and with them
the corridors, get longer: a mean loss of 4.7 edges at N = 8 becomes 17.7 at
N = 16, where the two score distributions separate outright (the control's
worst seed scored 373; the corridor arm's best managed 365). Widening the
corridor to a 2-cell ribbon, which lets its second rank land at 2 contacts
instead of 1, roughly halves the damage at every size, the direction the
scheduling argument predicts.

## What this rules out, and what it leaves open

The negative is scoped: for this greedy producer, at this budget, on these
generated instances, laying 1-wide (or 2-wide) clue-to-clue corridors as an
early commitment cost 3 to 18 edges, and more corridor cost more. It says
nothing yet about backtrackers, SAT or CP solvers, or the official board. The
same arithmetic points at where constraint is real: cells placed with 2 or
more contacts, since only $k \ge 3$ prunes outright. Growing compact
clue-anchored regions, where most cells arrive with several contacts, is a
different move and is untouched by this experiment.

The result also fits what the hint studies keep finding. On a related 16×16
puzzle, [scattered hints beat contiguous top rows](/research/why/hint-geometry);
and in [the lab hint study](/research/lab/experiments/raphael-anjou/hint-study),
the five official clues alone never helped a chronological backtracker on the
boards tested. Like the negatives in
[the theorem sweep](/research/why/theorem-sweep), this one comes priced: for
clue linking to pay, its cells would have to arrive with three contacts, and
a 1-wide path never does.
