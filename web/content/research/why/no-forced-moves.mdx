Every one of the 196 interior pieces has between 73 and 137 other pieces that
can legally sit to its right (counting right-hand partners, as the chart above
does). The typical piece has over a hundred options. With only one neighbour
known, no piece is ever pinned to one choice, so a single-edge constraint never
forces a move.

That statement is about one edge at a time. A cell in a real search usually
knows more: under the standard top-left fill it knows both its north and its
west colour. Then the candidate set collapses to about three pieces on
average, and 47 of the 269 possible (north, west) demands have exactly one
server (see [piece theft](/research/why/piece-theft)). Forced moves do exist;
they arrive late, once two sides are fixed, and cannot be chained from a
single edge.

The spread is also not a design signature. Run the same count on 100 randomly
painted 16x16 boards with the same palette (5 frame colours, 17 interior
colours, the engine's framed generator) and the median piece has between 120
and 128 partners (Eternity II: 125), the minimum ranges from 36 to 79
(Eternity II: 73), and no board has a forced piece. The number follows from
the colour frequencies alone: 17 colours shown in four rotations leave about
120 compatible partners per piece.

## Why it matters

Put this beside forbidden patterns and the shape of the difficulty appears.
Pairwise the puzzle looks loose: any piece fits next to plenty of others, so
propagating a single edge prunes almost nothing. Globally almost every
combination is illegal. The pruning that does happen comes from cells with
two or more fixed neighbours, which is why forward checking and
[arc consistency](/research/build/reduce/arc-consistency) work on the
conditional domains, not on pairwise counts. A solver still makes a long run
of free-looking choices whose consequences only show up once later cells
close around them. Nothing here is special to Eternity II: it is the generic
behaviour of a 16x16 edge-matching puzzle with this many colours.
