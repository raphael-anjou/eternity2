The best public boards have clustered in the high 460s for years, and the
ceiling stands at 470 of 480 (the record history lives on the
[records page](/research/records)). The question this page asks is whether
those last ten points are an engineering problem, something a better
backtracker or a sharper heuristic will eventually claw back, or a property
of the instance itself. The record history so far favours engineering: every
point gained since 2008 came from solver engineering and compute (a retuned
fast backtracker and weeks of many-core time for 468 to 470, a modified
search and a distributed fleet for the strict-track gains). This page
conjectures, without proof, that this source of progress is near exhaustion,
and sets out what the piece set can and cannot say about it.

The argument has two layers with very different standing, and I keep them
separate throughout. The numbers computed on the real piece set are exact,
and the compute step behind this page reproduces them bit for bit. The
structural picture that interprets those numbers at 16×16 is a conjecture,
motivated by an analogy with random constraint-satisfaction problems and by
an unpublished exploratory enumeration of small instances, and it is labeled
as such below.

## The bag, measured exactly

The 256 pieces split into 4 corners, 56 edge pieces, and 196 interior pieces.
Setting aside the 64 gray rim half-edges leaves 960 colored half-edges, which
pair into the 480 internal adjacencies of a filled board. The accounting is
tight, $960 = 2 \times 480$, as it is for any puzzle cut from a painted
solved board. Scores on this page count matched internal adjacencies out of
480, the same matched-edges convention as the community ceiling; nothing here
is a claim about the strict five-clue track.

The color economy then splits into two subsystems that never talk to each
other. The frame ring, the cycle of 60 joints between border pieces, uses
only colors 1 to 5, each appearing on exactly 24 half-edges; the probability
that two uniformly drawn frame half-edges agree is
$p_f = 5 \cdot (24/120)^2 = 0.200$. The interior subsystem covers the other
420 joints on colors 6 to 22 (five colors at 48 half-edges, twelve at 50),
giving $p_i \approx 0.0588$, almost exactly $1/17$. These two probabilities
carry the whole analysis.

One more exact check is worth recording: the real bag contains zero tiles
that repeat under rotation. Against random solved boards painted with the
same colour counts this is unusual (about 4 such pairs on average, and none
in only about 1.4% of painted sets), so it is the likeliest trace of a
deliberate design pass; the comparison is on the
[piece-set page](/research/why/why-e2-is-hard).

## One number places the regime

The parameter that positions Eternity II inside its ensemble is the
constraint density: the expected number of tiles that fit a fully constrained
interior cell, one whose four neighbours are already placed. With 196
interior tiles, 4 rotations each, and a per-edge collision probability of
0.0589 for a random interior tile edge,

$$\mu = 196 \cdot 4 \cdot 0.0589^4 \approx 0.0094.$$

A fully surrounded hole admits, besides the piece the planted solution puts
there, about one extra candidate in a hundred. That $\mu$ is far below 1 is
not specific to this puzzle: it holds for any such palette above about 5.3
equiprobable interior colours ($784\,p^4 < 1$ once $p < 0.189$). By analogy
with random CSPs, where shattering of the solution space and overlap-gap
barriers are proven for models such as random k-SAT and graph colouring
([Achlioptas & Coja-Oghlan 2008](https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.2122),
[Gamarnik 2021](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2108492118)), one may
conjecture a shattered solution space here too. No theorem covers planted
edge matching with an all-different constraint; the number is exact, the
regime it implies is the conjectural layer.

## The annealed score landscape

The instance-side centerpiece is a first-moment count: how many board
configurations that are *uncorrelated* with the planted solution reach a
given score? The base count of class-respecting placements (corners in
corners, edges on the rim, interiors inside, free rotations for interior
tiles) is $W_{\text{geom}} = 4! \cdot 56! \cdot 196! \cdot 4^{196} \approx
10^{559.9}$. The score of a random such configuration is modelled as the sum
of 60 independent Bernoulli($0.200$) frame indicators and 420 independent
Bernoulli($0.0588$) interior indicators, and an exact log-space convolution
of those 480 variables gives the full landscape. A uniformly random
configuration scores $36.7 \pm 5.7$.

| score | uncorrelated configurations at this score (log10) |
| ----: | ------------------------------------------------: |
|    37 |                                              558.8 |
|   200 |                                              462.8 |
|   400 |                                              186.6 |
|   460 |                                               59.6 |
|   465 |                                               46.7 |
|   470 |                                               33.1 |
|   475 |                                               18.4 |
|   478 |                                                8.7 |
|   480 |                                              +1.28 |

The curve falls smoothly: about 2.5 decades per point near 460, about 3 near
475, with no break, kink or gap anywhere in the 460s or 470s. It does not
single out 470. Taken at face value it predicts about $10^{18}$ uncorrelated
configurations at 475 and about $10^{9}$ at 478, so on its own it predicts
boards above the record, not a wall at it. Entropy is also not
accessibility: $10^{33}$ boards at 470 out of $10^{560}$ is a fraction near
$10^{-527}$, and a count says nothing about whether a search can reach them.

The top row needs a stronger caveat. The count crosses 1 essentially at 480
itself, $10^{1.28} \approx 19$, but that is the naive independent-edge first
moment. It includes the planted board's three global rotations (overlap zero
with it, so "uncorrelated" in this model), and it ignores the parity and
depletion corrections that the
[solution-counting page](/research/build/analysis/solution-counting) shows
to be worth nearly six orders of magnitude: the same naive product with the
starter fixed gives 0.02 where the corrected estimate is about 14,702, and
the corrected estimate without the starter is about $1.15 \times 10^{7}$.
The 19 here is used only for the shape of the curve, never as a solution
count, and a first moment does not bound the actual number of solutions of
this one puzzle anyway.

## Exact below, conjecture above

Everything above this line is an exact computation on the official set: the
class counts, the 960-half-edge economy, both collision probabilities, $\mu$,
$W_{\text{geom}}$, and every row of the landscape table. The repro command on
this page regenerates all of it from the shared engine in under a second,
into `results/landscape.json` in the topic folder. What is exact is the
arithmetic; the independent-edge model it evaluates is an approximation, as
the paragraph above prices.

What the landscape does not tell you is how those rare high scorers are
*arranged*: whether the entropic mass connects to the perfect boards or
whether an empty gap separates them. An unpublished exploratory enumeration
of small planted boards, with the color count tuned so the constraint
density matches, suggested that the overlap-with-planted histogram of the
solution set turns from continuous to bimodal as $\mu$ drops toward E2's
0.009, with an empty band between the planted cluster and a family at
overlap zero. Its code and per-instance histograms are not committed, so it
is a pointer for future work, not evidence this page relies on.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Where the conjecture starts">
  The 16×16 statements (an empty overlap gap below the perfect boards, and
  the 470 plateau as the visible edge of that gap) are a conjecture, not
  measurement: the landscape table does not locate a gap, and no feasible
  computation checks one directly at full size. The table and every
  instance-side number on this page are exact; the gap structure at 16×16 is
  the part to hold as an open hypothesis.
</Callout>

## Reading the plateau

Put the two layers side by side and they make a hypothesis, not a verdict.
A heuristic that climbs the score landscape draws from the entropic band,
which the table says is still deep at 470. The conjecture is that, past
some score in the high 470s, the boards a stable local search can reach stop
connecting to the perfect ones: the missing points would then be the width
of an empty overlap region, the overlap gap property in its textbook role.
Whether such a gap exists here, and where, is exactly what is open. The
measured history cuts the other way: the last record points came from more
throughput and better search engineering, so the plateau has not yet been
shown to be a property of the instance rather than of the methods tried.

The [rigidity wall](/research/why/rigidity-wall) is compatible with the
conjecture without proving it: record boards are strict local optima under
every bounded-radius refill tested, which is how the bottom edge of a gap
would feel from below, and also how any deep local optimum feels. If the gap
exists, crossing it would need correlation with an actual perfect solution
rather than more climbing of the score function. The statements about the
instance that hold at proof grade, as opposed to the working model on this
page, are collected in the [theorem sweep](/research/why/theorem-sweep).
