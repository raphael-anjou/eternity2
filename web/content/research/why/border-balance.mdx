Look only at the seam between the outer ring of border pieces and the first
ring of interior pieces. Each edge across that seam shows one colour, counted
once from the border side and once from the interior side. In any complete
solution the two tallies are identical: the multiset of colours the border
presents inward exactly equals the multiset the interior presents outward.

That is not a hidden law; it follows from the definition of a solved board.
Every seam edge in a solution shows the same colour from both sides, so the
two tallies agree term by term, and they agree for every solution of every
puzzle, the four known full solutions included. Call the imbalance the
**deficit**, $\Delta$: half the total mismatch between the two tallies. It is
a lower bound on the number of mismatched seam edges, since each mismatched
seam edge can move at most one unit of colour out of balance on each side.

## The identity, in one line

Across the border↔interior seam, write $A[c]$ and $B[c]$ for the per-colour
tallies seen from the border and from the interior. On any board with all 56
seam edges matched,

$$
\Delta \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{c} \bigl|\, A[c] - B[c] \,\bigr| \;=\; 0,
\qquad\text{and in general}\qquad
\Delta \;\le\; \#\{\text{mismatched seam edges}\}.
$$

One more fact makes it weaker than it looks. Each of the 56 edge pieces has
exactly one inward side, whatever its position, and the corners have none, so
on a complete frame the border tally $A$ is fixed by the piece set alone. Only
$B$, the colours the first interior ring turns outward, can vary.

## See it on a real board

A solved 8×8 starts in balance ($\Delta = 0$). Lift a border piece and watch
which colours fall out of balance, and the deficit climb. Then try the swap
button, and watch the catch.

## The catch

Swapping two border pieces leaves $\Delta$ at 0. The swap moves colours around
the seam without changing either tally, so the deficit is blind to it while
the seam may now hold mismatches. On a near-solution most of the remaining
errors are not on the seam at all. They sit interior-to-interior, where the
deficit never looks. Across the nine bundled 469-class boards, of the 99
unmatched edges that remain, 86.9% sit interior-to-interior and not one is
border-to-border; only the other 13.1% land on the seam. Counting those seam
mismatches directly is both cheaper and sharper than computing $\Delta$.

## Is it useful, then?

Not as stated. In a backtracker that only places pieces matching their placed
neighbours, every seam edge with both sides placed already matches, so
$\Delta$ over the placed cells is identically zero and rejects nothing. The
version that could prune compares the border's remaining seam demand with
what the unused interior pieces can still supply, colour by colour. That is a
counting (global-cardinality) constraint on the remaining pool, the family
constraint programming handles with global cardinality constraints; it is not
defined or benchmarked on this site yet, so its pruning value on Eternity II
is open.
